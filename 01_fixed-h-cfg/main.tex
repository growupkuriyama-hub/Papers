\begin{abstract}
We study distributional learning of context-free languages under a fixed
recognizable congruence $\sim_h$, the kernel of a homomorphism
$h:\Sigma^*\to M$ into a finite monoid: nonempty factors with equal $h$-values
that share one context must share all contexts.  For fixed $h$ we define a
set-driven (sample-set based) reconstruction operator that recovers every
context-free $\sim_h$-substitutable target exactly from finitely many positive
witnesses, and a conservative sequential learner that identifies every
nonempty such target in the limit; reconstruction and updates take polynomial
time.  The size of the canonical witnesses is controlled by a finite
target-side refinement parameter introduced later, and this parameter can be
exponentially larger than the corresponding source-grammar thickness.  For
one fixed two-element typing, no sample-set based reconstruction scheme valid
for the whole slice admits characteristic data bounded polynomially in reduced
CFG size and source-grammar thickness.  By contrast, such characteristic data
are polynomially bounded for every fixed
prefix--suffix window $(k,\ell)$, whose typing captures Yoshinaka's
$(k,\ell)$-substitutability exactly, and polynomial in the size of a linear
CFG for linear targets under every fixed $h$.  For suitable fixed homomorphisms we exhibit regular, nonregular linear, and
nonlinear context-free targets that lie outside every fixed-window class, and
we show that the one-bracket Dyck language lies outside every finite-typing
slice.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Background and motivation}

Gold~\cite{gold1967} showed that no language class containing all finite
languages and at least one infinite language is identifiable in the limit
from positive data alone. This negative result motivated a broad line of
research in grammatical inference; see, for example,
de~la~Higuera~\cite{higuera2010} for general background. For context-free languages, one standard way to
recover positive learning results is the \emph{distributional} approach,
in which learnability is obtained by restricting which substrings may be
compared across contexts. Classical examples include Clark--Eyraud
substitutability~\cite{clark-eyraud2007} and Yoshinaka's
$(k,\ell)$-substitutability~\cite{yoshinaka2008}. More generally,
positive-data learnability has also been established for other grammar
classes under suitable finite control assumptions; see, for example,
Kanazawa~\cite{kanazawa1998} for restricted categorial grammars.

The present paper studies a recognizable-congruence version of this
distributional program. Let $h:\Sigma^*\to M$ be a homomorphism into a
finite monoid, and write $\sim_h:=\ker h$, that is, $x\sim_h y$ iff $h(x)=h(y)$. For $x\in\Sigma^*$, let
$\D_L(x):=\{(u,v)\in\Sigma^*\times\Sigma^*:uxv\in L\}$ denote its
distribution in $L$. A language $L\subseteq\Sigma^*$ is
\emph{$\sim_h$-substitutable} if $h(x)=h(y)$ and
$\D_L(x)\cap\D_L(y)\neq\emptyset$ imply $\D_L(x)=\D_L(y)$ for all
$x,y\in\Sigma^+$%
\footnote{Clark--Eyraud treat $a^+$ as substitutable, whereas including $\lambda$ itself among the compared fragments would exclude $a^+$. See also Clark~\cite{clark2013}.}. Thus full equality of distributions is required only
for strings that already agree under a fixed finite algebraic summary.

Recognizable congruences are a natural comparison mechanism here. If
$L=h^{-1}(\mathsf{Acc})$ for some finite monoid homomorphism $h:\Sigma^*\to M$ and
some $\mathsf{Acc}\subseteq M$, then any two strings with the same $h$-value
automatically have the same continuation behavior, and hence the same
distribution. Thus every regular language belongs to the broader
class $\mathsf{RS}$ (defined formally in Section~\ref{sec:framework}) of
languages that are $\sim_h$-substitutable for \emph{some} finite monoid
homomorphism. This observation shows that finite
monoid typings are not an ad hoc generalization of bounded contexts, but a
canonical algebraic source of a finite comparison bias.

Our focus is the \emph{fixed-$h$} regime: the same finite monoid
homomorphism $h:\Sigma^*\to M$ is fixed uniformly over the class
$\Ccf{h}$ of context-free $\sim_h$-substitutable languages, rather than
selected separately for each target after observing its data.  Thus fixed $h$
plays the same class-level role as a fixed pair $(k,\ell)$ in Yoshinaka's
hierarchy.  We separate choosing a useful typing
from learning once that typing has been specified, a viewpoint
methodologically close to the use of externally supplied typing or domain
background knowledge in automata inference~\cite{coste-typing2004}.
Coste et al. encode expert typing by finite-state type automata: the type of
an occurrence may depend on its left and right contexts, and state merging is
constrained so that the typing embedded in the inferred automaton remains
compatible with that expert typing.  Our object is different---$h$ gives a
compositional finite type to terminal factors rather than an occurrence-level
contextual type to automaton structure---but it plays an analogous role as a
fixed finite comparison bias.
Finite biases can also be composed without making the typing target-specific:
for example, the product of a fixed window typing with an independently
specified finite domain feature is again one fixed finite-monoid typing for
the whole class; Section~\ref{sec:framework} makes this precise.

There is also a useful contrast with control-set approaches.  Takada's 1988
result reduces inference of even-linear languages to inference of regular
control sets on a fixed universal even-linear grammar~\cite{takada1988}.
His 1995 synthesis makes the architecture especially explicit: on a fixed
universal unambiguous even-linear grammar, each target has a unique canonical
regular control set, and learning even-linear languages reduces to learning
regular languages; the same chapter develops regular-control representations
more broadly for linear and equal-matrix grammars~\cite{takada1995}.  In
these approaches the target-specific regular control language constrains
sequences of production labels.  By contrast, the present homomorphism acts
on terminal yields and supplies a class-level finite \emph{comparison bias}:
fixing $h$ restricts which substrings may be compared but does not determine
one target language.  Related positive-data work obtains learnable subclasses
by restricting the regular control sets further
~\cite{koshiba-makinen-takada1997}.  We use this contrast only for
positioning; no equivalence, subsumption, or class separation with respect to
the control-set methodology in general is claimed.

\subsection{Contributions and main results}

The paper has three main contributions.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\arabic*)},leftmargin=2.4em]
\item \emph{Learning for fixed $h$.}  A set-driven reconstruction operator
$\mathcal B_h$ reconstructs every target in $\Ccf{h}$ exactly from finitely
many canonical positive witnesses, and a separate conservative sequential
learner $\mathcal A_h$ identifies every nonempty target in Gold's sense;
reconstruction and updates take time polynomial in the accumulated sample.

\item \emph{Quantitative boundary.}  For arbitrary fixed $h$, the size of
the canonical witnesses is controlled by a finite parameter of a target-side
refinement introduced in Section~\ref{sec:typed-thickness}; this parameter can
be exponentially larger than the corresponding source-grammar thickness.
For one fixed two-element typing $h_\circ$, no reconstruction operator whose
output depends only on the observed sample set and that has characteristic
samples throughout $\Ccf{h_\circ}$ admits a bound polynomial in reduced CFG
size and source-grammar thickness.  Such a bound does hold for every fixed
prefix--suffix window $(k,\ell)$, whose typing captures Yoshinaka's
$(k,\ell)$-substitutability exactly, and data polynomial in linear-grammar
size suffice for linear targets under every fixed $h$.

\item \emph{Expressive position.}  Suitable fixed homomorphisms admit
regular, nonregular linear, and nonlinear context-free targets outside every
fixed-window class.  The one-bracket Dyck language lies outside
$\mathsf{RS}$ altogether, and $\mathsf{RS}\cap\mathrm{CFL}$ is properly
contained in Clark's congruential family.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:prelim}

Let $\Sigma$ be a finite alphabet, let $\Sigma^*$ be the free monoid with
identity $\lambda$, and put $\Sigma^+:=\Sigma^*\setminus\{\lambda\}$.
A pair $(u,v)\in\Sigma^*\times\Sigma^*$ is called a
\emph{terminal context}.  For $L\subseteq\Sigma^*$ and $x\in\Sigma^*$, the \emph{distribution} of $x$ in $L$ is
$\D_L(x):=\{(u,v)\in\Sigma^*\times\Sigma^*:uxv\in L\}$.
The \emph{syntactic congruence} of $L$ is defined by $x\equiv_L y$ iff
$\D_L(x)=\D_L(y)$, for $x,y\in\Sigma^*$.
We say that $x$ and $y$ are \emph{syntactically congruent} when
$x\equiv_L y$, and write $[x]_{\equiv_L}$ for the $\equiv_L$-equivalence
class of $x$.

A context-free grammar (CFG) is a tuple $G=(V,\Sigma,P,S)$ with finite
nonterminal set $V$, start symbol $S\in V$, and productions
$A\to\alpha$ with $A\in V$ and $\alpha\in(V\cup\Sigma)^*$.
A \emph{sentential form} is any word in $(V\cup\Sigma)^*$.  We write
$\alpha\Rightarrow_G\beta$ for one derivation step in $G$, and
$\Rightarrow_G^*$ for its reflexive-transitive closure.  Thus $L(G):=\{w\in\Sigma^*:S\Rightarrow_G^*w\}$.  For $A\in V$, put
$L_G(A):=\{w\in\Sigma^*:A\Rightarrow_G^*w\}$.
For a nonterminal $X\in V$, a terminal context $(u,v)$ is a
\emph{terminal reaching context of $X$ in $G$} if
$S\Rightarrow_G^*uXv$.
A nonterminal is \emph{reachable} if it occurs in a sentential form reachable
from $S$, and \emph{productive} if it derives a terminal word.  A nonterminal
is \emph{useless} if it is not both reachable and productive, and it is
\emph{nullable} if it derives $\lambda$.  A production $A\to B$ with
$A,B\in V$ is a \emph{unit production} (or \emph{unit rule}).  The
\emph{unit closure} is the reflexive-transitive closure of the relation on
nonterminals induced by unit productions.  A grammar is \emph{reduced} if
every nonterminal is reachable and productive.  For a grammar generating a
nonempty language, its \emph{trim} (or \emph{reduced part}) is the subgrammar
obtained by deleting all useless nonterminals together with their incident
productions.  A CFG is \emph{linear} if every right-hand side contains at
most one nonterminal.  By \emph{binarization} we mean replacement of longer
right-hand sides by equivalent chains of binary productions using fresh
nonterminals.
We write $\mathrm{CFL}$ for the class of context-free languages.  For any
finite representation $r$, $|r|$ denotes the length of a fixed reasonable
encoding; for a grammar this is polynomially equivalent to the usual symbol
count of its productions.  Following Yoshinaka's fixed-window analysis, where this grammar parameter is attributed to Wakatsuki and Tomita~\cite{yoshinaka2008,wakatsuki-tomita1993}, for a reduced grammar $G=(V,\Sigma,P,S)$ we use the \emph{thickness} parameter
\[
 \tau_G:=\max_{A\in V}\min\{\,|w|:A\Rightarrow_G^*w,\ w\in\Sigma^*\,\}.
\]

\subsection{Learning from positive data}\label{sec:learning}

We use Gold's explanatory model~\cite{gold1967}.  Let $H_0$ denote a fixed
CFG with one start symbol and no productions, so $L(H_0)=\emptyset$.  We use
texts only for nonempty targets and represent the empty language separately by
$H_0$; equivalently one may adjoin the usual pause symbol.  For a nonempty language $L$, a \emph{text} is an
infinite sequence $T=(w_1,w_2,\ldots)$ over $L$ in which every word of $L$
occurs.  Write $T[n]=(w_1,\ldots,w_n)$ and
$K_n:=\{w_1,\ldots,w_n\}$.  A learner $\mathcal A$
\emph{identifies a nonempty $L$ in the limit} if for every text $T$ for $L$
there are $n_0$ and a grammar $H$ such that $\mathcal A(T[n])=H$ for every
$n\ge n_0$ and $L(H)=L$.
Thus the hypothesis representation itself eventually stabilizes.  A
sequential learner is \emph{conservative} if it changes its current hypothesis
only when the newly presented positive datum is not generated by that
hypothesis.

We distinguish this sequential learner from a set-driven finite-sample
operator $\mathcal B$, where $\mathcal B(K)$ depends only on the finite set
$K$, not on presentation order or multiplicity.  We call $\mathcal B$
\emph{sample-consistent} if $K\subseteq L(\mathcal B(K))$ for every finite
sample $K$.  A finite set $C\subseteq L$ is a \emph{characteristic sample for
$\mathcal B$} if
$C\subseteq K\subseteq L$ implies $L(\mathcal B(K))=L$ for every finite
$K$.  This is the positive-data specialization of the characteristic-sample
component in de~la~Higuera's polynomial grammatical-inference
framework~\cite{higuera1997}.  We measure sample size by
$\|K\|:=\sum_{w\in K}(|w|+1)$%
\footnote{The $+1$ prevents a nonempty sample such as $K=\{\lambda\}$ from having size $0$; it also gives $|K|\le\|K\|$, so the number of examples is controlled by the same measure.},
so $\lambda$ contributes one unit; the quantitative results below combine
polynomial-time construction of $\mathcal B(K)$ with polynomial-size
characteristic data.

\subsection{Fixed finite-monoid typing}\label{sec:fixed-typing}

Fix a finite monoid $M$ and a homomorphism $h:\Sigma^*\to M$, represented
by the multiplication table of $M$ and the letter images $h(a)$ for
$a\in\Sigma$.  Thus $h(w)$ is computable by a left-to-right product in
$O(|w|)$ time; this is the sense in which the fixed homomorphism is
\emph{efficiently computable} below.

\begin{definition}[fixed-$h$ substitutability]\label{def:hsubst}
A language $L\subseteq\Sigma^*$ is \emph{$\sim_h$-substitutable} if for all
$x,y\in\Sigma^+$, equality $h(x)=h(y)$ together with
$\D_L(x)\cap\D_L(y)\ne\varnothing$ implies $\D_L(x)=\D_L(y)$.
\end{definition}
The empty word is not used as an internal substitutable fragment.  When
$\lambda\in L$, the reconstruction handles it separately through the start
symbol.

For a nonempty internal factor $x$, we call $h(x)$ its
\emph{yield type}.  We write $\Ccf{h}$ for the context-free
$\sim_h$-substitutable languages and define
$\Clin{h}:=\{L\in\Ccf{h}:L\text{ is generated by some linear CFG}\}$.
The polynomial data bound below is measured relative to a chosen linear CFG
representation of the target; the reconstruction hypothesis produced later
need not itself be linear.

\section{Fixed Recognizable Substitutability}
\label{sec:framework}

For the fixed homomorphism $h$, put
$\mathsf{RS}_h:=\{L\subseteq\Sigma^*:L\text{ is $\sim_h$-substitutable}\}$.
Thus $\Ccf{h}=\mathsf{RS}_h\cap\mathrm{CFL}$, while $\Clin{h}$ consists of
those languages in $\mathsf{RS}_h\cap\mathrm{CFL}$ that are generated by
some linear CFG.
For expressive comparisons only, let
$\mathsf{RS}:=\bigcup_h\mathsf{RS}_h$, where $h$ ranges over finite-monoid
homomorphisms with domain $\Sigma^*$.  All such class notation is relative to
the ambient alphabet.  When the alphabet must be displayed explicitly, we
write $\mathsf{RS}_h(\Gamma)$ and $\mathsf{RS}(\Gamma)$ for the analogous
classes over $\Gamma^*$; class inclusions are always understood
alphabetwise.  The learning results in this paper concern one fixed $h$.
For every nonempty alphabet, neither $\mathsf{RS}$ nor
$\mathsf{RS}\cap\mathrm{CFL}$ is identifiable from positive data.\footnote{By
Proposition~\ref{prop:regular-auto}, every regular language belongs to
$\mathsf{RS}$. Hence both $\mathsf{RS}$ and $\mathsf{RS}\cap\mathrm{CFL}$
contain every finite language over the ambient alphabet and also the infinite
regular language $\Sigma^*$. Thus both classes are superfinite, and Gold's
theorem~\cite{gold1967} applies.}  There is, however, a simple monotonicity
with respect to refinement of the typing bias.  If $h':\Sigma^*\to M'$ refines $h$ in the sense that
$\ker h'\subseteq\ker h$, then
$\mathsf{RS}_h\subseteq\mathsf{RS}_{h'}$: equality under $h'$ implies
equality under $h$, so every comparison required by $h'$ is already covered by
$h$-substitutability.  Thus finer typings permit a larger fixed-$h$ class by
requiring fewer pairs of factors to be compared.  This gain is not cost-free
in the quantitative bounds below, since refining the typing may increase the
number of typed nonterminals and hence the witness-set size.  In particular,
for two finite typings $h$ and $g$, the product homomorphism $h\times g$ has
$\ker(h\times g)=\ker h\cap\ker g$ and therefore refines both; hence
$\mathsf{RS}_h\cup\mathsf{RS}_g\subseteq\mathsf{RS}_{h\times g}$.  Thus
window information and an independent finite domain-specific feature can be
combined in a single fixed typing.

A second useful observation gives a simple sufficient condition on the typing.
If $\ker h$, restricted to $\Sigma^+$, refines $\equiv_L$, then%
\footnote{If one required $\ker h\subseteq\equiv_L$ on all of $\Sigma^*$, the condition would be too strong: for the trivial monoid, $\lambda$ and every nonempty word have the same type, so even the ordinarily substitutable language $a^+$ would be excluded because $\lambda\not\equiv_{a^+}a$.}
$L\in\mathsf{RS}_h$, since equal $h$-types are already syntactically congruent.
The converse fails already for ordinary substitutability.  Clark--Eyraud's
language $L_0=\{a^ncb^n:n\ge0\}$ is substitutable~\cite[Section~6.1]{clark-eyraud2007},
hence belongs to the trivial-monoid slice, but $\equiv_{L_0}$ has infinite
index: if $1\le r<s$, then $(a,cb^{r+1})$ is a context of $a^r$ but not of
$a^s$.  Thus fixed-$h$ substitutability is strictly weaker than requiring
$\ker h$ to refine the full syntactic congruence.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:regular-auto}
A language is regular iff it has the form $h^{-1}(\mathsf{Acc})$ for some homomorphism
$h:\Sigma^*\to M$ into a finite monoid $M$ and some $\mathsf{Acc}\subseteq M$.  For every fixed
$h$ and $\mathsf{Acc}\subseteq M$, the language $h^{-1}(\mathsf{Acc})$ is
$\sim_h$-substitutable.  Thus every regular language belongs to
$\mathsf{RS}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The first statement is the standard finite-monoid characterization of regular
languages~\cite{eilenberg1974}.  If $L=h^{-1}(\mathsf{Acc})$ and $h(x)=h(y)$,
then $h(uxv)=h(uyv)$ for every $u,v$; hence $\D_L(x)=\D_L(y)$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Classical special cases}

Clark--Eyraud's Definition~5~\cite{clark-eyraud2007} is written for
all strings, but their Section~6.1 treats $a^+$ as substitutable; Clark's later
formulation~\cite{clark2013} explicitly uses nonempty compared fragments.  We
follow this operational convention: a language $L\subseteq\Sigma^*$ is called
\emph{Clark--Eyraud substitutable} here if, for all $x,y\in\Sigma^+$,
$\D_L(x)\cap\D_L(y)\ne\varnothing$ implies $\D_L(x)=\D_L(y)$.
Under Definition~\ref{def:hsubst}, this is exactly the trivial-monoid case and
hence also Yoshinaka's $(0,0)$ case.

\begin{proposition}[Clark--Eyraud special case]\label{prop:ce-special}
Every Clark--Eyraud substitutable language belongs to $\mathsf{RS}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Use the one-element monoid.
\end{proof}

For $k,\ell\ge0$, write $\pref_k(w)$ and $\suf_\ell(w)$ for the prefix and
suffix of the indicated lengths whenever they exist, with
$\pref_0(w)=\suf_0(w)=\lambda$.

Yoshinaka's original definition~\cite{yoshinaka2008} writes the left
length-$k$ window as $v\in\Sigma^k$ and the right length-$\ell$ window as
$u\in\Sigma^\ell$.  To avoid collision with our generic context notation
$(u,v)$, we rename only these two window variables to $p$ and $q$ below.
Thus a language $L$ is \emph{$(k,\ell)$-substitutable} if for all
$p\in\Sigma^k$, $q\in\Sigma^\ell$ and strings
$x_1,x_2,y_1,y_2,z_1,z_2\in\Sigma^*$, with $py_1q,py_2q\ne\lambda$,
\[
\begin{split}
&x_1py_1qz_1\in L,\quad x_1py_2qz_1\in L,\quad
x_2py_1qz_2\in L\\
&\hspace{35mm}\Longrightarrow\quad x_2py_2qz_2\in L.
\end{split}
\]

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:yl-special}
For every fixed $k,\ell\ge0$ there is a finite monoid homomorphism
$h_{k,\ell}$ such that, for every language $L\subseteq\Sigma^*$,
$L$ is $(k,\ell)$-substitutable if and only if it is
$\sim_{h_{k,\ell}}$-substitutable.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Put $m_0:=\max\{1,k+\ell\}$, let $h_{k,\ell}(w):=w$ if $|w|<m_0$ and
$h_{k,\ell}(w):=\langle\pref_k(w),\suf_\ell(w)\rangle$ (with a tag
separating short words from pairs) otherwise, and let $M_{k,\ell}$ be the
image.  For short words $x,y$ and tagged pairs define
\[
\begin{aligned}
x\cdot y&:=h_{k,\ell}(xy),&
x\cdot\langle p,q\rangle&:=\langle\pref_k(xp),q\rangle,\\
\langle p,q\rangle\cdot y&:=\langle p,\suf_\ell(qy)\rangle,&
\langle p,q\rangle\cdot\langle p',q'\rangle&:=\langle p,q'\rangle.
\end{aligned}
\]
The four cases give $h_{k,\ell}(xy)=h_{k,\ell}(x)\cdot h_{k,\ell}(y)$;
surjectivity of $h_{k,\ell}$ then gives associativity, with identity
$h_{k,\ell}(\lambda)$.

For $s,t\in\Sigma^+$, equal $h_{k,\ell}$-types mean either $s=t$ or that
$s=py_1q$ and $t=py_2q$ for some $p\in\Sigma^k$ and
$q\in\Sigma^\ell$ (for $k=\ell=0$ every nonempty word has the same tagged
pair).

First suppose that $L$ is $(k,\ell)$-substitutable.  In the nontrivial case
above, the first two premise words in Yoshinaka's condition say that $s,t$
share a context, the third gives an arbitrary context of $s$, and the
conclusion transfers that context to $t$.  Quantifying over the context and
using symmetry gives $\D_L(s)=\D_L(t)$.

Conversely, suppose that $L$ is $\sim_{h_{k,\ell}}$-substitutable and take
an instance of Yoshinaka's premise.  Put $s:=py_1q$ and $t:=py_2q$.  These
two factors are nonempty by definition.  Moreover, if $k+\ell>0$ then
$|s|,|t|\ge k+\ell=m_0$, while if $k=\ell=0$ their nonemptiness gives
$|s|,|t|\ge1=m_0$.  Hence both are in the long-word part of the coding and
$h_{k,\ell}(s)=\langle p,q\rangle=h_{k,\ell}(t)$.  The first two premise
words give a shared context for $s,t$, so their distributions are equal; the
third premise word is then a context of $s$ and therefore also of $t$, which
is exactly the required fourth word.
\end{proof}

\phantomsection\label{crit:kl}
\paragraph{Fixed-window counterexample criterion.}
Let $k,\ell\ge0$ and put $m_0:=\max\{1,k+\ell\}$.  If
$s,t\in\Sigma^*$ satisfy $|s|,|t|\ge m_0$,
$\pref_k(s)=\pref_k(t)$, $\suf_\ell(s)=\suf_\ell(t)$,
$\D_L(s)\cap\D_L(t)\ne\varnothing$, and $\D_L(s)\ne\D_L(t)$,
then $L$ is not $(k,\ell)$-substitutable.  Indeed, the length and
prefix--suffix assumptions give $h_{k,\ell}(s)=h_{k,\ell}(t)$, whereas the
shared context together with unequal distributions shows that $L$ is not
$\sim_{h_{k,\ell}}$-substitutable.  Proposition~\ref{prop:yl-special}
therefore gives the claim.

Consequently the fixed $(k,\ell)$ class is exactly
$\mathsf{RS}_{h_{k,\ell}}$.  Section~\ref{sec:fixed-window-thick} shows that
the present reconstruction has a finite-sample bound polynomial in grammar
size and ordinary thickness on this special case.  For
$k=\ell=0$, the homomorphism $h_{0,0}$ separates $\lambda$ from the nonempty
words, so under Definition~\ref{def:hsubst} it induces the same
substitutability condition as the one-element trivial monoid.  For an
alphabet $\Gamma$, define
\[
\mathsf{KL}(\Gamma):=
\bigcup_{k,\ell\ge0}
\{L\subseteq\Gamma^*:L\text{ is $(k,\ell)$-substitutable}\},
\]
and abbreviate $\mathsf{KL}:=\mathsf{KL}(\Sigma)$ on the current alphabet.

\subsection{Main theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Fixed-$h$ learning theorem]\label{thm:main}
Fix an efficiently computable homomorphism $h:\Sigma^*\to M$ into a finite
monoid.  There are a set-driven reconstruction operator $\mathcal B_h$ and a
conservative sequential learner $\mathcal A_h$ such that:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)},nosep]
\item $\mathcal B_h$ is sample-consistent and $\mathcal B_h(K)$ is
constructible in time polynomial in $\|K\|$;
\item every $L\in\Ccf{h}$ has a finite characteristic sample for
$\mathcal B_h$;
\item $\mathcal A_h$ identifies every nonempty $L\in\Ccf{h}$ in Gold's
sense, each update taking time polynomial in the data seen so far;
\item for fixed $(k,\ell)$, taking $h=h_{k,\ell}$, the sample in~(ii) can
be chosen polynomial in $|R|$ and $\tau_R$ for any reduced CFG $R$
representing the target;
\item for arbitrary fixed $h$ and $L\in\Clin{h}$, the sample in~(ii) can be
chosen polynomial in $|R|$ for any linear CFG $R$ representing $L$.
\end{enumerate}
The quantitative mechanism behind items~(iv)--(v), together with its
limitation for arbitrary fixed typings, is developed in
Sections~\ref{sec:typed-thickness}--\ref{sec:linear}.
\end{theorem}

The class $\Ccf{h}$ does not contain all finite languages, in accordance
with Gold's theorem: choose distinct nonempty $x,y$ with $h(x)=h(y)$ and
$|r|>\max\{|x|,|y|\}$.  Then $x,y$ share the empty context in
$\{x,y,rx\}$ while $(r,\lambda)$ is a context of $x$ only, so this finite
language is not $\sim_h$-substitutable.

\section{Reconstruction Operator and Sequential Learner}
\label{sec:algo}


\subsection{Finite-sample reconstruction}
For a finite sample $K\subseteq\Sigma^*$, let
$\widehat V(K):=\{[x:u,v]:uxv\in K,\ x\in\Sigma^+\}$.
These are syntactic triples: $[x:u,v]=[x':u',v']$ iff
$x=x'$, $u=u'$, and $v=v'$; different observed factors or contexts therefore
give different hypothesis nonterminals.  We call the elements of
$\widehat V(K)$ \emph{observed nonterminals}.
The grammar $\mathcal B_h(K)=\widehat G(K)$ has nonterminal set
$\widehat V(K)\cup\{\widehat S\}$ and start symbol $\widehat S$.  It has the
following productions whenever every displayed bracketed nonterminal belongs
to $\widehat V(K)$:
\[
\begin{array}{rll}
(R1)&[xy:u,v]\to[x:u,yv][y:ux,v],&x,y\in\Sigma^+,\\[1mm]
(R2)&[x:u,v]\to[x:u',v'],&uxv,u'xv'\in K,\\[1mm]
(R3)&[x:u,v]\to[x':u,v],&h(x)=h(x'),\\[1mm]
(R4)&[a:u,v]\to a,&a\in\Sigma,\\[1mm]
(R5)&\widehat S\to[w:\lambda,\lambda],&w\in K\cap\Sigma^+.
\end{array}
\]
If $\lambda\in K$, Rule~(R5) also contains $\widehat S\to\lambda$.
Intuitively, $[x:u,v]$ represents possible replacements for the observed
factor $x$ in the context $(u,v)$.  For a $\sim_h$-substitutable target, the
soundness invariant below ensures that every derived word has the same
$h$-type as $x$ and is distributionally interchangeable with it.
Rule~(R2) transports the same observed factor between contexts in which
it has been observed; no condition on the $h$-types of those surrounding
contexts is needed.  Rule~(R3) changes the internal representative within a
fixed yield type, preserving the yield-type invariant used by soundness.

\begin{lemma}[sample consistency]\label{lem:sample-consistency}
For every finite sample $K$, $K\subseteq L(\mathcal B_h(K))$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If $w=a_1\cdots a_m\in K\cap\Sigma^+$, Rule~(R5) enters
$[w:\lambda,\lambda]$.  Repeated use of Rule~(R1) along any binary
factorization of this occurrence of $w$ reaches the one-letter factors, and
every intermediate observed nonterminal exists because it is a factor of the
same sample word with its induced context.  Rule~(R4) then emits
$a_1\cdots a_m$.  If $\lambda\in K$, the start rule emits it directly.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[soundness]\label{thm:soundness}
If $K\subseteq L$ and $L$ is $\sim_h$-substitutable, then
$L(\widehat G(K))\subseteq L$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Induct on the height of a derivation tree rooted at $[x:u,v]$ to prove
\begin{equation}\label{eq:soundness-invariant}
 [x:u,v]\Rightarrow_{\widehat G(K)}^*w
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 uwv\in L\ \text{ and }\ h(w)=h(x).
\end{equation}
Rules~(R1)--(R4) contain no erasing production, and every lexical rule emits
one terminal.  Hence every terminal word derived from a non-start hypothesis
nonterminal is nonempty; in particular, the words $w,w_1,w_2$ to which
Definition~\ref{def:hsubst} is applied below lie in $\Sigma^+$.
Rule~(R4) is immediate.  For Rule~(R3), the induction hypothesis gives
$h(w)=h(x')=h(x)$ and already gives $uwv\in L$.  For Rule~(R2), the
induction hypothesis gives $u'wv'\in L$ and $h(w)=h(x)$.  Since
$[x:u',v']$ and $[x:u,v]$ belong to $\widehat V(K)$, its definition gives
$u'xv',uxv\in K\subseteq L$.  Thus $x$ and $w$ share the context
$(u',v')$, so substitutability gives $\D_L(x)=\D_L(w)$ and hence
$uwv\in L$.

For Rule~(R1), write the derived word as $w_1w_2$.  The two induction
hypotheses give
\[
 uw_1yv\in L,\qquad uxw_2v\in L,\qquad
 h(w_1)=h(x),\qquad h(w_2)=h(y).
\]
Since $uxyv\in K$, the strings $x$ and $w_1$ share context $(u,yv)$ and
have the same type; hence their distributions agree.  By the second induction
hypothesis, $(u,w_2v)\in\D_L(x)$.  Applying the distribution equality to this
context gives $uw_1w_2v\in L$; also $h(w_1w_2)=h(xy)$ by the induction hypotheses.
This proves~\eqref{eq:soundness-invariant}.  Start rules then give the claim for $L(\widehat G(K))$;
$\lambda$ is present only when
$\lambda\in K\subseteq L$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The sequential learner}

Recomputing $\mathcal B_h$ after every datum need not stabilize
syntactically.\footnote{For $L=\{a^ncb^n:n\ge0\}$, longer examples add
new triples such as $[a^ncb^n:\lambda,\lambda]$; see also
Eyraud--Heinz--Yoshinaka~\cite[Appendix]{eyraud-heinz-yoshinaka2016} on the
gap between set-based and incremental learning.}
We therefore use the conservative wrapper of Clark--Eyraud's SGL
(Algorithm~2) and Yoshinaka's $(k,\ell)$-SGL
(Algorithm~1)~\cite{clark-eyraud2007,yoshinaka2008}.  Starting from the fixed
empty grammar $H_0$, after reading $w_n$ put
\[
 H_n:=
 \begin{cases}
 H_{n-1},&w_n\in L(H_{n-1}),\\
 \mathcal B_h(K_n),&w_n\notin L(H_{n-1}),
 \end{cases}
 \qquad
 \mathcal A_h(T[n]):=H_n.
\]
Every rebuild uses the whole accumulated sample $K_n$.  Although
$\mathcal B_h$ is set-driven, the grammar on which $\mathcal A_h$ stabilizes
may depend on the presentation order; only eventual syntactic stabilization
to a grammar for the target is claimed (Corollary~\ref{cor:ilt}).

\paragraph{Order dependence.}
For infinite targets this dependence can be seen directly.  Let
$L\in\Ccf{h}$ be infinite and let $T$ be a text on which $\mathcal A_h$
is constant from stage $n_0$ onward.  Choose $w\in L$ longer than every
word in $K_{n_0}$, which is possible because an infinite language over a
finite alphabet has unbounded word length.  The limit grammar on $T$ is
$\mathcal B_h(K_m)$ for some last rebuild stage $m\le n_0$ and therefore
has no nonterminal $[w:\lambda,\lambda]$.  On the text obtained by placing
$w$ first and then following $T$, the first hypothesis is
$\mathcal B_h(\{w\})$, and every later rebuild uses an accumulated sample
still containing $w$; hence every later hypothesis contains
$[w:\lambda,\lambda]$.  The two stabilized grammars are therefore
syntactically different although both eventually generate $L$.  The
same language $\{a^ncb^n:n\ge0\}$ is an instance of this phenomenon.

\section{Completeness via Yield-Typed Refinement and Finite Witnesses}
\label{sec:typing}


\subsection{Target refinement and finite witnesses}

For the completeness proof only, we refine a target grammar by yield type and
show that the hypothesis can mimic finitely witnessed typed derivations.  We use
the following normal form as a proof device.

\begin{definition}[start-separated binary normal form]
A CFG $G=(V,\Sigma,P,S_0)$ is in \emph{start-separated binary normal form}
(SSBNF) if $S_0$ never occurs on a right-hand side%
\footnote{This isolates whole-sentence entry from internal nonterminals, simplifying the typed refinement and the special treatment of $\lambda$.},
every start rule is $S_0\to A$ or $S_0\to\lambda$, and every non-start rule
is either $A\to a$ or $A\to BC$.
\end{definition}

Every nonempty context-free language has an equivalent reduced SSBNF grammar by
the standard start-separation, useless-symbol elimination, $\lambda$/unit elimination
away from the start, and binarization procedures; the case of the empty
target language is immediate.

Let $G=(V,\Sigma,P,S_0)$ be a reduced SSBNF grammar for
$L\in\Ccf{h}$.  Its \emph{full yield-typed refinement} has start symbol
$\widetilde S$ and non-start symbols $A_\mu$ with
$A\in V\setminus\{S_0\}$ and $\mu\in M$.  Here and below, juxtaposition of type indices denotes multiplication in
$M$; thus $\mu\nu=\mu\cdot\nu$.  The rules are
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
\widetilde S\to A_\mu & (S_0\to A\in P,\ \mu\in M),\\
\widetilde S\to\lambda & (S_0\to\lambda\in P),\\
A_{h(a)}\to a & (A\to a\in P),\\
A_{\mu\nu}\to B_\mu C_\nu & (A\to BC\in P,\ \mu,\nu\in M).
\end{array}
\]
The \emph{typed refinement}
$\widetilde G=(\widetilde V,\Sigma,\widetilde P,\widetilde S)$ is the
reduced part of this grammar.  By convention, every symbol of
$\widetilde G$ is therefore reachable and productive.  The yield-type index
is needed for Rule~(R3) in the completeness proof: without it, for a target
rule $A\to BC$, the canonical yield $\omega(A)$ and the concatenation
$\omega(B)\omega(C)$ need not have the same $h$-value, so the required
Rule~(R3) transition need not exist.  Splitting by yield type makes this
equality an invariant.  By contrast, no types are attached to the
surrounding left or right contexts, because the reconstruction operator never
tests such types.

\begin{proposition}[typed lifting and yield invariant]\label{prop:typed-core}
The typed refinement satisfies $L(\widetilde G)=L(G)$.  Moreover, if $X=A_\mu$ is a non-start symbol of $\widetilde G$, then
$X\Rightarrow_{\widetilde G}^*w$ implies $h(w)=\mu$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Erase type annotations to map every typed derivation to a derivation of $G$.
Conversely, annotate each non-start node of a successful derivation tree of
$G$ by the $h$-type of its terminal yield.  The terminal and binary rules
above are exactly the bottom-up propagation laws for those types, so the
annotated tree is a derivation of the full refinement; all symbols on a
successful tree survive trimming.  The invariant follows by induction on a
typed derivation.
\end{proof}

Fix an order on $\Sigma$ and use the induced \emph{shortlex} order:
shorter strings come first, with lexicographic order breaking equal-length
ties.  For every non-start symbol $X$ of $\widetilde G$, let $\omega(X)$
be its shortlex-minimal terminal yield, called the \emph{canonical yield} of $X$.  Choose $\chi(X)=(u_X,v_X)$ among the terminal reaching contexts of $X$ in
$\widetilde G$ so as to minimize $|u_X|+|v_X|$, breaking ties by the
shortlex orders of $u_X$ and then $v_X$; call $\chi(X)$ the \emph{canonical context} of $X$.  Such a terminal reaching context
exists because $\widetilde G$ is reduced:
reachability places $X$ in a reachable sentential form, and productivity of
the surrounding nonterminals lets all symbols to its left and right be
expanded to terminal strings.  Proposition~\ref{prop:typed-core} gives
$h(\omega(A_\mu))=\mu$ for every typed non-start symbol.

Define the finite witness set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:W}
\begin{split}
\WitnessSet(\widetilde G):={}&
 \{u_X\omega(X)v_X:X\in\widetilde V\setminus\{\widetilde S\}\}\\
&\cup\{u_Xav_X:X\to a\in\widetilde P\}\\
&\cup\{u_X\omega(Y)\omega(Z)v_X:X\to YZ\in\widetilde P\}\\
&\cup\bigl(\{\lambda\}\text{ if }\lambda\in L\bigr).
\end{split}
\end{equation}
Every displayed word belongs to $L$, and the set is finite.  In particular,
for a binary rule $X\to YZ$ the corresponding witness is justified by
$\widetilde S\Rightarrow^*u_XXv_X\Rightarrow u_XYZv_X
\Rightarrow^*u_X\omega(Y)\omega(Z)v_X$; anchors and terminal-rule
witnesses are analogous.  We call the
elements of $\WitnessSet(\widetilde G)$ the \emph{canonical positive witnesses}.
Here the word $u_X\omega(X)v_X$ is the \emph{anchor} of $X$, witnessing one canonical
occurrence of $X$, while the other words serve the distinct role of terminal or binary
\emph{rule witnesses}.


\subsection{Exact reconstruction and Gold convergence}

\begin{theorem}[completeness from the finite witnesses]\label{thm:complete}
Let $G$ be any reduced SSBNF grammar for a nonempty language $L$, and let
$\widetilde G$ be its typed refinement with respect to the fixed
homomorphism $h$.  If a finite set $K$ satisfies
$\WitnessSet(\widetilde G)\subseteq K$, then $L\subseteq L(\widehat G(K))$.
\end{theorem}

The statement is purely combinatorial: it uses the typed grammar and
$\WitnessSet(\widetilde G)\subseteq K$, but neither substitutability nor the
additional assumption $K\subseteq L$.

\begin{proof}
For each non-start $X$ write $\widehat X:=[\omega(X):u_X,v_X]$.
The anchor word in~\eqref{eq:W} ensures that $\widehat X$ exists.  We prove
by induction on a typed derivation $X\Rightarrow^*w$ that
$\widehat X\Rightarrow_{\widehat G(K)}^*w$.

If the first rule is $X\to a$, the witness $u_Xav_X\in K$ gives
$[a:u_X,v_X]$.  If $\omega(X)\ne a$, Rule~(R3) changes $\widehat X$ to
$[a:u_X,v_X]$ because $h(\omega(X))=h(a)$; if $\omega(X)=a$, this step is
unnecessary.  Rule~(R4) then yields $a$.

Suppose the first rule is $X=A_{\mu\nu}\to B_\mu C_\nu$; put
$Y:=B_\mu$ and $Z:=C_\nu$.
The rule witness in~\eqref{eq:W} gives the three observed nonterminals
needed to apply Rule~(R1), yielding
\begin{equation}\label{eq:binary-reconstruction}
 [\omega(Y)\omega(Z):u_X,v_X]
 \to[\omega(Y):u_X,\omega(Z)v_X]
      [\omega(Z):u_X\omega(Y),v_X].
\end{equation}
Because $h(\omega(X))=\mu\nu=h(\omega(Y)\omega(Z))$, Rule~(R3), if
necessary, changes $\widehat X$ to the left-hand side
of~\eqref{eq:binary-reconstruction}; when $\omega(X)=\omega(Y)\omega(Z)$,
this step is unnecessary.  Rule~(R2) then transports
the two children to $\widehat Y$ and $\widehat Z$, which are determined by
the canonical contexts of $Y$ and $Z$.  The induction hypotheses finish the two subderivations.

Finally, every nonempty $w\in L$ has a lifted start derivation
$\widetilde S\to X\Rightarrow^*w$.  The start rule itself shows that
$(\lambda,\lambda)$ is a terminal reaching context of $X$; its total length is $0$,
which is minimal, so the canonical choice is
$\chi(X)=(\lambda,\lambda)$.  Its anchor $\omega(X)$ belongs to $K$, so
Rule~(R5) starts the preceding derivation.  The case $w=\lambda$ is immediate
from~\eqref{eq:W} and Rule~(R5).
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[exact finite-sample reconstruction]
\label{thm:reconstruction-fixed-h}
Let $L\in\Ccf{h}$ be nonempty, let $G$ be a reduced SSBNF grammar for $L$,
and let $\widetilde G$ be its typed refinement.  If
$\WitnessSet(\widetilde G)\subseteq K\subseteq L$, then $L(\mathcal B_h(K))=L$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Combine Theorems~\ref{thm:soundness} and~\ref{thm:complete}.
\end{proof}

Thus, for every nonempty target representation $G$, the set
$\WitnessSet(\widetilde G)$ is a characteristic sample for $L(G)$
with respect to $\mathcal B_h$ in the sense of Section~\ref{sec:learning}.

Theorem~\ref{thm:reconstruction-fixed-h} covers every nonempty target,
including $\{\lambda\}$.  For the remaining endpoint $L=\emptyset$, take
$C_L=\emptyset$; then $\mathcal B_h(\emptyset)$ has no start production.
Together these cases establish item~(ii) of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.

\begin{corollary}[Gold identification]\label{cor:ilt}
For a fixed homomorphism $h:\Sigma^*\to M$ into a finite monoid $M$, the learner
$\mathcal A_h$ identifies every nonempty language in $\Ccf{h}$ from positive data.  More precisely, for any reduced SSBNF target
grammar $G$ and its typed refinement $\widetilde G$, once
$\WitnessSet(\widetilde G)\subseteq K_{n_0}$, among stages $n\ge n_0$ at most one hypothesis change
can occur.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Under our text convention the Gold statement concerns nonempty targets;
$H_0$ is the initial empty hypothesis.  Let $L$ be nonempty and let $T$ be a
text for $L$.  Since $w_1\notin L(H_0)=\emptyset$, the learner rebuilds at
stage $1$.  Choose $n_0$ with
$\WitnessSet(\widetilde G)\subseteq K_{n_0}$.  If the learner rebuilds at
some stage $n\ge n_0$, Theorem~\ref{thm:reconstruction-fixed-h} makes the
new hypothesis exactly $L$, after which no positive example can trigger
another change.  In this case that rebuild is the last hypothesis change.
Suppose instead that no rebuild occurs at any stage $n\ge n_0$.  Soundness
gives $L(H_{n_0})\subseteq L$.  Moreover, $K_n\subseteq L(H_n)$ for every
$n$: a rebuild is sample-consistent, while at a non-rebuild step the newly
presented datum is already generated by the unchanged hypothesis.  Hence a
strict inclusion $L(H_{n_0})\subsetneq L$ would leave some
$w^\star\in L\setminus L(H_{n_0})$ that has not yet occurred by stage
$n_0$.  Since every target word eventually occurs in the text, $w^\star$
must occur later and force a rebuild, a contradiction.  Thus
$L(H_{n_0})=L$; in this case no hypothesis change after stage $n_0$ is
needed.
\end{proof}

This is the delayed-rebuild phenomenon highlighted by
Eyraud--Heinz--Yoshinaka~\cite[Appendix]{eyraud-heinz-yoshinaka2016}:
after a characteristic sample has been seen, a conservative learner can
still retain an earlier incorrect hypothesis if every newly seen example is
already generated by it; a later positive example outside that hypothesis
forces a rebuild from an accumulated sample that already contains the
characteristic data.  The proof above spells out this stabilization argument
for $\mathcal B_h$.

\section{Complexity of the Reconstruction Step}
\label{sec:complexity}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:poly-build}
For fixed $h$, $\mathcal B_h(K)$ is constructible in time polynomial in
$\|K\|$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Put $n_K:=\|K\|$.  Sample words have $O(n_K^2)$ factorizations $uxv$ with
$x\ne\lambda$, so $|\widehat V(K)|=O(n_K^2)$, and all factor $h$-values can
be cached with $O(n_K^2)$ multiplications in the fixed monoid.  Rule~(R1) has
$O(n_K^3)$ candidates and Rules~(R2)--(R3) at most $O(n_K^4)$.  A polynomial
preprocessing step can assign canonical identifiers to equal factors and
context components (for example by sorting the $O(n_K^2)$ factor
occurrences); once these identifiers and the $h$-values are cached, each
subsequent equality/type test is constant-time for fixed $h$.  Each production
has encoding length $O(n_K)$, so the grammar can
be checked and written explicitly in $O(n_K^5)$ time.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:poly-update}
At each stage the conservative learner $\mathcal A_h$ can process the next
positive example in time polynomial in the encoded size of the data seen so
far.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
CFG membership is polynomial in the grammar and input sizes, and every current
hypothesis has polynomial size by Theorem~\ref{thm:poly-build}; a triggered
rebuild is polynomial by the same theorem.
\end{proof}

Together with Lemma~\ref{lem:sample-consistency},
Theorem~\ref{thm:reconstruction-fixed-h} and Corollary~\ref{cor:ilt}, this
proves Theorem~\ref{thm:main}(i)--(iii).  The quantitative bounds in
items~(iv)--(v) are proved in Sections~\ref{sec:fixed-window-thick}--\ref{sec:linear}.
No bound in grammar size alone is possible: the reduced SSBNF grammars
$S_0\to A_n$, $A_0\to a$, $A_i\to A_{i-1}A_{i-1}$ have $O(n)$
production symbols but generate only $a^{2^n}$, and every singleton language
is $\sim_h$-substitutable for every $h$.

\section{Typed Thickness for General Fixed Typings}
\label{sec:typed-thickness}

\subsection{Typed-thickness bounds and a presentation-level gap}

For an arbitrary fixed $h$, the finite-witness construction admits a direct
quantitative bound once the cost of yield typing is measured explicitly.  Let
$G=(V,\Sigma,P,S_0)$ be a reduced SSBNF grammar and let $\widetilde G$ be its
trimmed yield-typed refinement.  Put
\[
 N:=|V\setminus\{S_0\}|,\qquad
 N_t:=|\widetilde V\setminus\{\widetilde S\}|,
\]
and define the \emph{typed thickness} of this presentation by
\[
 \tau_h^{\mathrm{typ}}(G):=
 \max\Bigl(\{\,|\omega(X)|:
 X\in\widetilde V\setminus\{\widetilde S\}\,\}\cup\{1\}\Bigr).
\]
Thus $\tau_h^{\mathrm{typ}}(G)$ measures shortest terminal yields after the
full yield-type split and trimming.  It is a presentation-dependent
parameter, not an additional assumption on the learner.

\begin{lemma}[typed-thickness witness bound]
\label{lem:typed-thickness-bound}
Let $G$ and $\widetilde G$ be as above and write
$\tau:=\tau_h^{\mathrm{typ}}(G)$.  Every non-start typed symbol $X$ has
\[
 |u_X|+|v_X|\le (N_t-1)\tau,
\]
and every word $w\in\WitnessSet(\widetilde G)$ satisfies
\[
 |w|\le (N_t+1)\tau.
\]
If $P_{\mathrm{ter}}$ and $P_{\mathrm{bin}}$ are respectively the terminal
and binary non-start productions of $G$, then
\[
 \|\WitnessSet(\widetilde G)\|
 \le
 \bigl(N|M|+|P_{\mathrm{ter}}|
       +|P_{\mathrm{bin}}||M|^2+1\bigr)
 \bigl((N|M|+1)\tau+1\bigr).
\]
In particular, for fixed $h$ this bound is polynomial in $|G|$ and
$\tau_h^{\mathrm{typ}}(G)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Form the directed dependency graph whose vertices are the non-start symbols of
$\widetilde G$ and whose edges join a parent to each child occurring in a
binary production.  Since $\widetilde G$ is reduced, every non-start symbol
$X$ is reachable.  Hence some start child $A$ has a path to $X$ in this
graph.  Choose a shortest such path.  It repeats no typed symbol, so it has at
most $N_t-1$ edges.

For each edge of the path, choose a witnessing binary production and expand
the child not lying on the path to its canonical yield.  Starting from
$\widetilde S\to A$, these expansions produce a terminal reaching context of
$X$.  Each off-path canonical yield has length at most $\tau$, so the total
context length is at most $(N_t-1)\tau$.  The canonical context
$(u_X,v_X)$ is no longer than this one.

An anchor adds one canonical yield, a terminal-rule witness adds one terminal,
and a binary-rule witness adds two canonical yields to the canonical context.
Since $\tau\ge1$, every word in~\eqref{eq:W} therefore has length at most
$(N_t+1)\tau$.  Finally, $N_t\le N|M|$; each source terminal production has
at most one typed terminal copy, and each source binary production has at
most $|M|^2$ typed copies.  Thus the displayed first factor bounds the number
of witnesses, including the possible witness $\lambda$, while the second
factor bounds $|w|+1$ for each witness.  Multiplying the two bounds proves the
claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[characteristic data from typed thickness]
\label{cor:typed-thickness-data}
Fix $h$.  If $G$ is a reduced SSBNF grammar for a nonempty
$L(G)\in\Ccf{h}$, then $\WitnessSet(\widetilde G)$ is a characteristic
sample for $\mathcal B_h$ whose encoded size is polynomial in
$|G|$ and $\tau_h^{\mathrm{typ}}(G)$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Lemma~\ref{lem:typed-thickness-bound} gives the quantitative bound, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:reconstruction-fixed-h} gives characteristicity.
\end{proof}

The typed thickness cannot in general be replaced by the ordinary source
thickness through the full-refinement argument.

\begin{remark}[exponential source-to-typed thickness gap]
\label{rem:typed-thickness-gap}
Typed thickness need not be polynomially bounded by ordinary source
thickness.  Let $M_{\mathtt c}=\{1,\zeta\}$ be the two-element monoid
with zero $\zeta$, let
$h_{\mathtt c}(\mathtt a)=1$ and $h_{\mathtt c}(\mathtt c)=\zeta$,
and for $n\ge1$ let $G_n^{\mathtt c}$ have
\[
\begin{aligned}
 S_0&\to Y, &
 Y&\to \mathtt a\mid\mathtt c\mid YY\mid A_nC, &
 C&\to\mathtt c,\\
 A_0&\to\mathtt a, &
 A_i&\to A_{i-1}A_{i-1}\mid\mathtt c
 \quad(1\le i\le n).
\end{aligned}
\]
These reduced SSBNF grammars have size $O(n)$, ordinary thickness $1$, and
language $\{\mathtt a,\mathtt c\}^+\in\Ccf{h_{\mathtt c}}$.
In the trimmed typed refinement, the only product equal to $1$ is
$1\cdot1$, so
$L((A_i)_1)=\{\mathtt a^{2^i}\}$ by induction.  The symbol $(A_n)_1$
survives trimming through
$\widetilde S\to Y_\zeta\to(A_n)_1C_\zeta$, and therefore
$\tau_{h_{\mathtt c}}^{\mathrm{typ}}(G_n^{\mathtt c})\ge2^n$.
Thus no polynomial bound on typed thickness in terms of source grammar size
and ordinary thickness is possible even for this fixed typing.  Since the
target language $\{\mathtt a,\mathtt c\}^+$ is independent of $n$, this
establishes only a gap for the canonical full-refinement witnesses;
Theorem~\ref{thm:ordinary-thickness-lower} gives the
operator-independent lower bound.
\end{remark}

\subsection{A set-driven ordinary-thickness lower bound}
\label{subsec:ordinary-thickness-lower}

We use two elementary observations.

\begin{lemma}[restriction to type fibres]
\label{lem:fibre-restriction}
Let $h:\Sigma^*\to M$ be a monoid homomorphism and let $L$ be
$\sim_h$-substitutable.  For every $F\subseteq M$, the language
$L\cap h^{-1}(F)$ is also $\sim_h$-substitutable.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $L_F:=L\cap h^{-1}(F)$ and equal-type nonempty factors $x,y$ share a
context in $L_F$, then they share one in $L$, so $\D_L(x)=\D_L(y)$.  For
all $u,v$,
\[
 uxv\in L_F
 \iff uxv\in L\ \text{and}\ h(u)h(x)h(v)\in F,
\]
and both conditions are unchanged when $x$ is replaced by $y$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[nested-target characteristic-sample obstruction]
\label{lem:nested-characteristic-obstruction}
Let $\mathcal B$ be any set-driven operator and let $L'\subsetneq L$.  If
$C$ and $C'$ are characteristic samples for $L$ and $L'$, respectively,
with respect to the same $\mathcal B$, then
\[
 C\cap(L\setminus L')\ne\varnothing.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Otherwise $C\subseteq L'$.  For $K:=C\cup C'$ we have
$C\subseteq K\subseteq L$ and $C'\subseteq K\subseteq L'$, so
characteristicity forces both $L(\mathcal B(K))=L$ and
$L(\mathcal B(K))=L'$, a contradiction.
\end{proof}

Fix
\[
 \Sigma_\circ:=\{\mathtt l,\mathtt r,\mathtt a,\mathtt c,
                    \mathtt 0,\mathtt d\}
\]
and the two-element monoid $M_\circ:=\{1,\zeta\}$ with identity $1$ and
zero $\zeta$.  This is the same abstract two-element zero monoid used in
Remark~\ref{rem:typed-thickness-gap}; the homomorphism is given a new
name because its alphabet and letter map are different.  Define
$h_\circ:\Sigma_\circ^*\to M_\circ$ by
$h_\circ(\mathtt c)=\zeta$ and by sending every other letter to $1$; thus
$h_\circ(w)=\zeta$ exactly when $w$ contains $\mathtt c$.

For $i\ge0$ put $s_i:=\mathtt c\mathtt 0^{\,i+1}\mathtt d$ and define
finite languages
\[
 \mathcal T_0:=\{\mathtt l\mathtt a\mathtt r,\,
                    \mathtt l s_0\mathtt r\},
 \qquad
 \mathcal T_i:=\{\mathtt l s_i\mathtt r\}
 \cup\{\mathtt lxy\mathtt r:x,y\in\mathcal T_{i-1}\}
 \quad(i\ge1).
\]
Let
\[
 z_0:=\mathtt l\mathtt a\mathtt r,
 \qquad
 z_i:=\mathtt l z_{i-1}z_{i-1}\mathtt r.
\]
Then $z_i$ is the unique $\mathtt c$-free word of $\mathcal T_i$, and
\begin{equation}
 |z_i|=5\cdot2^i-2.
 \label{eq:clean-tree-length}
\end{equation}

The tree-parsing argument needed for the lower bound is elementary but
notationally longer than the main line of the proof, so we defer its details to
Appendix~\ref{app:level-coded-substitutability}.  The key point is that every
word of $\mathcal T_n$ has a unique residual-height tree parse and is obtained
from the clean tree $z_n$ by replacing an antichain of clean subtrees by the
level-coded shortcuts $s_i$.  The level code makes the replacement sites
unambiguous.

\begin{lemma}[level-coded tree languages are substitutable]
\label{lem:level-coded-substitutable}
For every $n\ge0$, $\mathcal T_n$ is substitutable in the
Clark--Eyraud sense.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
See Appendix~\ref{app:level-coded-substitutability}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[set-driven ordinary-thickness lower bound]
\label{thm:ordinary-thickness-lower}
For the fixed homomorphism $h_\circ$ above and every $n\ge1$ there are
reduced CFGs $R_n,R_n^-$ such that, with
$\mathcal T_n^-:=\mathcal T_n\setminus\{z_n\}$,
\[
 L(R_n)=\mathcal T_n,\qquad L(R_n^-)=\mathcal T_n^-,
\]
\[
 \mathcal T_n,\mathcal T_n^-\in\Ccf{h_\circ},\qquad
 |R_n|+|R_n^-|=\operatorname{poly}(n),\qquad
 \tau_{R_n},\tau_{R_n^-}\le n+5.
\]
Let $\mathcal B$ be any set-driven operator for which both targets have
characteristic samples.  Then every characteristic sample $C_n$ for
$\mathcal T_n$ satisfies
\[
 \|C_n\|\ge5\cdot2^n-1.
\]
Consequently no set-driven operator admitting characteristic samples for all
targets in $\Ccf{h_\circ}$ has a characteristic-data bound polynomial in
reduced CFG size and ordinary thickness.  In particular this applies to
$\mathcal B_{h_\circ}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
A compact reduced CFG $R_n$ is
\[
\begin{aligned}
 S&\to A_n,&
 Z_0&\to\mathtt0,&
 Z_i&\to Z_{i-1}\mathtt0 &&(1\le i\le n),\\
 A_0&\to\mathtt l\mathtt a\mathtt r
       \mid\mathtt l\mathtt cZ_0\mathtt d\mathtt r,\\
 A_i&\to\mathtt lA_{i-1}A_{i-1}\mathtt r
       \mid\mathtt l\mathtt cZ_i\mathtt d\mathtt r
       &&(1\le i\le n).
\end{aligned}
\]
It generates exactly $\mathcal T_n$.  Every displayed symbol is reachable
and productive, and the production-symbol count is $O(n)$; hence the encoded
size is polynomial in $n$ under our fixed convention.  The shortcut
alternative gives $|\omega(A_i)|\le i+5$, so
$\tau_{R_n}\le n+5$.

For $R_n^-$ retain $Z_0,\ldots,Z_n$ and the rules for
$A_0,\ldots,A_{n-1}$ above, add $S^-\to A_n^-$, and put
\[
\begin{aligned}
 A_0^-&\to\mathtt l\mathtt cZ_0\mathtt d\mathtt r,\\
 A_i^-&\to\mathtt l\mathtt cZ_i\mathtt d\mathtt r
 \mid\mathtt lA_{i-1}^-A_{i-1}\mathtt r
 \mid\mathtt lA_{i-1}A_{i-1}^-\mathtt r
 \quad(1\le i\le n).
\end{aligned}
\]
Inductively, $A_i^-$ generates exactly the words of $\mathcal T_i$
containing at least one shortcut, so $L(R_n^-)=\mathcal T_n^-$.  This grammar
is again reduced, has $O(n)$ production symbols and polynomial encoded size,
and $|\omega(A_i^-)|\le i+5$, hence $\tau_{R_n^-}\le n+5$.

By Lemma~\ref{lem:level-coded-substitutable}, $\mathcal T_n$ is
$\sim_{h_\circ}$-substitutable.  Moreover
\[
 \mathcal T_n^-
 =\mathcal T_n\cap h_\circ^{-1}(\{\zeta\}),
\]
so Lemma~\ref{lem:fibre-restriction} gives the same property for
$\mathcal T_n^-$.  Both languages are finite and therefore belong to
$\Ccf{h_\circ}$.

Let $C_n$ and $C_n^-$ be characteristic samples for the two targets with
respect to $\mathcal B$.  Since
$\mathcal T_n\setminus\mathcal T_n^-=\{z_n\}$,
Lemma~\ref{lem:nested-characteristic-obstruction} forces $z_n\in C_n$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:clean-tree-length} gives
$\|C_n\|\ge|z_n|+1=5\cdot2^n-1$, which is exponential in $n$ while the
two displayed representation parameters are linear.
\end{proof}

\section{Fixed-Window Thick Data}
\label{sec:fixed-window-thick}

Both positive quantitative regimes below are obtained by bounding
$\tau_h^{\mathrm{typ}}$ and invoking
Corollary~\ref{cor:typed-thickness-data}.  For $h_{k,\ell}$, a typed
nonterminal need preserve only a bounded prefix and suffix, which controls
typed thickness; Remark~\ref{rem:typed-thickness-gap} shows that no
such transfer is available for general $h$.  Characteristic-sample bounds
polynomial in representation size and thickness are the data component of
identification in polynomial time and thick data (IPTtD) in the terminology
of Eyraud--Heinz--Yoshinaka~\cite{eyraud-heinz-yoshinaka2016}.  Yoshinaka's
Theorem~1 and Lemmas~6--7 give such bounds for
$(k,\ell)$-substitutability~\cite{yoshinaka2008}.  We prove the corresponding
bound for $\mathcal B_{h_{k,\ell}}$; neither the same normal form nor the
same polynomial degree is claimed.

Fix $k,\ell\ge0$, put $r:=k+\ell$, and let $G=(V,\Sigma,P,S_0)$ be a
reduced SSBNF grammar.  Write
\[
 N:=|V\setminus\{S_0\}|,\qquad
 N_t:=|\widetilde V\setminus\{\widetilde S\}|,\qquad
 \theta_{k,\ell}(G):=
 \begin{cases}
   \tau_G, & r=0,\\[1mm]
   r+(2r-1)N\tau_G, & r>0,
 \end{cases}
\]
where $\widetilde G$ is the reduced $h_{k,\ell}$-typed refinement.

\begin{lemma}[bounded fixed-window typed yields]
\label{lem:window-typed-yield}
Every non-start typed symbol $A_\mu$ of $\widetilde G$ satisfies
\[
 |\omega(A_\mu)|\le\theta_{k,\ell}(G).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
See Appendix~\ref{app:fixed-window-bounds}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[bounded fixed-window contexts and witnesses]
\label{lem:window-context}
For every non-start typed symbol $X$,
\[
 |u_X|+|v_X|\le(N_t-1)\theta_{k,\ell}(G),
\]
and every word in $\WitnessSet(\widetilde G)$ has length at most
$(N_t+1)\theta_{k,\ell}(G)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Combine Lemma~\ref{lem:window-typed-yield} with
Lemma~\ref{lem:typed-thickness-bound}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[polynomial thick data for fixed windows]
\label{thm:window-thick}
For every fixed pair $(k,\ell)$ and ambient alphabet $\Sigma$, there is a
polynomial $Q_{k,\ell}$ such that every reduced SSBNF grammar $G$ with
$L(G)\in\Ccf{h_{k,\ell}}$ satisfies
\[
 \|\WitnessSet(\widetilde G)\|
 \le Q_{k,\ell}(|G|,\tau_G),
\]
and $\WitnessSet(\widetilde G)$ is a characteristic sample for
$\mathcal B_{h_{k,\ell}}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Lemma~\ref{lem:window-typed-yield} gives
$\tau_{h_{k,\ell}}^{\mathrm{typ}}(G)\le\theta_{k,\ell}(G)$, and for
fixed $k,\ell,\Sigma$ this is polynomial in $|G|$ and $\tau_G$.
Corollary~\ref{cor:typed-thickness-data} now gives the claim.
\end{proof}

The theorem is stated for reduced SSBNF representations.  The following
normalization transfers it to arbitrary reduced CFGs.

\begin{proposition}[polynomial thickness-preserving SSBNF normalization]
\label{prop:thick-ssbnf-normal}
There are fixed polynomials $P_1$ and $P_2$ such that every reduced CFG
$R$ generating a nonempty language can be converted in polynomial time into
an equivalent reduced SSBNF grammar $G$ satisfying
\[
 |G|\le P_1(|R|),\qquad
 \tau_G\le P_2(|R|,\tau_R+1).
\]
\end{proposition}

The proof is in Appendix~\ref{app:thick-ssbnf}; terminal isolation and
binarization precede non-start $\lambda$-elimination so that grammar size and
shortest nonempty yields remain polynomially bounded.

\begin{corollary}[fixed-window complexity transfer]
\label{cor:window-transfer}
For every fixed $k,\ell\ge0$ and finite alphabet $\Sigma$, every
$L\in\Ccf{h_{k,\ell}}$ represented by a reduced CFG $R$---equivalently,
every context-free $(k,\ell)$-substitutable target represented by such an
$R$---has characteristic data for $\mathcal B_{h_{k,\ell}}$ whose encoded
size is polynomial in $|R|$ and $\tau_R$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Apply Proposition~\ref{prop:thick-ssbnf-normal} and
Theorem~\ref{thm:window-thick}; the equivalence is
Proposition~\ref{prop:yl-special}.
\end{proof}

The polynomial depends on the fixed window and alphabet; no uniform bound in
variable $k,\ell$ is asserted.  This is a characteristic-data statement for
the batch reconstruction; Gold stabilization of the conservative sequential
learner is Corollary~\ref{cor:ilt}.

\paragraph{Consistency with the general lower bound.}
Theorem~\ref{thm:ordinary-thickness-lower} does not conflict with the
fixed-window result.  The larger target $\mathcal T_n$ is ordinarily
substitutable, but for $n\ge1$ the nested target $\mathcal T_n^-$ is not:
write $z_n=p_n\mathtt a q_n$ with $p_n:=\mathtt l^{\,n+1}$ and obtain
$\bar q_n$ by replacing one other height-$0$ body $\mathtt a$ by $s_0$.
Then $p_n\mathtt a$ and $p_ns_0$ share the context
$(\lambda,\bar q_n)$ in $\mathcal T_n^-$, whereas $(\lambda,q_n)$ is
a context of $p_ns_0$ but not of $p_n\mathtt a$.  More generally, for
every fixed $(k,\ell)$, $\mathcal T_n^-\notin\Ccf{h_{k,\ell}}$ for all
sufficiently large $n$: otherwise the nested-target lemma together with
Corollary~\ref{cor:window-transfer} would force the exponentially long
$z_n$ into a polynomially bounded fixed-window characteristic sample.
The general lower bound is measured against arbitrary reduced CFG
representations and therefore also does not conflict with the IPTtD result
of Coste--Nicolas~\cite{coste-nicolas2019} for $k,\ell$-local
substitutable languages represented in RNF, where both the language
condition and representation class are different.

\section{The Linear Subclass}
\label{sec:linear}

Linear languages are exactly the languages accepted by one-turn pushdown
automata~\cite{ginsburg-spanier1966,autebert1997}.  The normalization below
gives the grammatical counterpart: every successful derivation has one
continuing nonterminal path, which we call its \emph{spine}.

\begin{proposition}[linear-spine SSBNF normalization]\label{prop:linear-normal}
There is a fixed polynomial $P_{\mathrm{lin}}$ such that every linear CFG
$G$ generating a nonempty language can be converted in polynomial time into
an equivalent reduced SSBNF grammar $G_0=(V_0,\Sigma,P_0,S_0)$ with
$|G_0|\le P_{\mathrm{lin}}(|G|)$ in which every binary production has
exactly one fresh terminal-wrapper child $W_a$ with $W_a\to a$ and exactly
one other nonterminal child.
\end{proposition}

The construction is given in Appendix~\ref{app:linear-normalization}.  Let
$\widetilde G_0$ be its typed refinement and put
$n_t:=|\widetilde V_0\setminus\{\widetilde S\}|$.  Since each underlying
non-start symbol has at most $|M|$ typed copies,
$n_t\le|V_0\setminus\{S_0\}|\,|M|$.

\begin{lemma}[short canonical yields]\label{lem:linear-short}
Every typed non-start symbol $X$ of $\widetilde G_0$ satisfies
\[
 |\omega(X)|\le n_t.
\]
Hence $\tau_h^{\mathrm{typ}}(G_0)\le n_t$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{theorem}[polynomial characteristic data for linear targets]
\label{thm:linear-poly}
For fixed $h$, every language in $\Clin{h}$ represented by a linear CFG has a
characteristic sample for $\mathcal B_h$ whose $\|\cdot\|$-size is
polynomial in the target representation.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For $L=\emptyset$ and $L=\{\lambda\}$ use the samples from
Section~\ref{sec:typing}.  Otherwise take $G_0$ from
Proposition~\ref{prop:linear-normal}.  Lemma~\ref{lem:linear-short} gives
$\tau_h^{\mathrm{typ}}(G_0)\le n_t\le
|V_0\setminus\{S_0\}|\,|M|$, and
Corollary~\ref{cor:typed-thickness-data} yields a characteristic sample
polynomial in $|G_0|\le P_{\mathrm{lin}}(|G|)$.
\end{proof}

Together with Theorem~\ref{thm:poly-build}, this gives polynomial-time batch
reconstruction from arbitrary finite samples and polynomial characteristic
data relative to a chosen linear CFG representation.  We do not label the
sequential learner itself as a de~la~Higuera polynomial time-and-data learner,
since $\mathcal B_h$ need not output a linear representation.

\subsection{A nonregular linear \texorpdfstring{fixed-$h$}{fixed-h} example}

The preceding theorem is not confined to regular targets or to previously
known linear substitutable classes.  Define
\[
\begin{split}
L_{\pm,e}:={}&
\{a^{2m}cb^{2m}:m\ge0\}
\cup
\{a^{2m+1}db^{2m+1}:m\ge0\}\\
&\cup
\{a^neb^n:n\ge0\}.
\end{split}
\]
It is generated by the linear grammar
\[
\begin{array}{lll}
S\to U_{\mathrm{ev}}\mid U_e, &
U_{\mathrm{ev}}\to c\mid aU_{\mathrm{odd}}b, &
U_{\mathrm{odd}}\to d\mid aU_{\mathrm{ev}}b,\\[1mm]
&&U_e\to e\mid aU_eb.
\end{array}
\]

Call $c,d,e$ the \emph{center symbols}.  Let
$M_{\pm,e}:=\{1,\kappa_{cd},\kappa_e,0\}$ be the four-element monoid in
which $1$ is the identity and $\mu\nu=0$ for all
$\mu,\nu\in\{\kappa_{cd},\kappa_e,0\}$.  Thus $M_{\pm,e}$ is obtained
by adjoining an identity to a three-element null semigroup.  Define the
homomorphism $h_{\pm,e}:\{a,b,c,d,e\}^*\to M_{\pm,e}$ by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lpm-h}
 h_{\pm,e}(a)=h_{\pm,e}(b)=1,\qquad
 h_{\pm,e}(c)=h_{\pm,e}(d)=\kappa_{cd},\qquad
 h_{\pm,e}(e)=\kappa_e.
\end{equation}
Hence $h_{\pm,e}(w)=1$ if $w$ contains no center symbol, $h_{\pm,e}(w)=\kappa_{cd}$ if it
contains exactly one center symbol and that symbol is $c$ or $d$,
$h_{\pm,e}(w)=\kappa_e$ if that unique center is $e$, and $h_{\pm,e}(w)=0$ if it contains at
least two center symbols.

Put $\operatorname{par}(c):=0$ and $\operatorname{par}(d):=1$.  Then, for
$m,n\ge0$ and $\xi\in\{c,d,e\}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lpm-membership}
a^m\xi b^n\in L_{\pm,e}
\iff
m=n
\ \text{and}\
\bigl[
 \xi=e
 \ \text{or}\
 (\xi\in\{c,d\}
  \ \text{and}\
  m\equiv\operatorname{par}(\xi)\pmod2)
\bigr].
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:linear-separator-example}
The language $L_{\pm,e}$ is linear and nonregular and is
$\sim_{h_{\pm,e}}$-substitutable for the homomorphism in~\eqref{eq:lpm-h}.  On the
other hand, it is not Clark--Eyraud substitutable and is not
$(k,\ell)$-substitutable for any fixed $k,\ell\ge0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Linearity is immediate from the displayed grammar, while
$L_{\pm,e}\cap a^*eb^*=\{a^neb^n:n\ge0\}$ proves nonregularity.  Write
$L:=L_{\pm,e}$.

For $\sim_{h_{\pm,e}}$-substitutability, let equal-type nonempty factors
$x,y$ share a context in $L$.  No factor occurring in a word of $L$ can have
type $0$.  If their common type is $1$, then
$x,y\in a^+\cup b^+$; the common context forces
$|x|_a-|x|_b=|y|_a-|y|_b$, which determines a word in
$a^+\cup b^+$ uniquely, so $x=y$.

Otherwise write $x=a^i\xi b^j$ and $y=a^{i'}\xi'b^{j'}$ with one center
symbol each.  By~\eqref{eq:lpm-membership}, every context of such a factor
has the form $(a^m,b^n)$, and
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
(a^m,b^n)\in\D_L(a^ieb^j)
 &\Longleftrightarrow n-m=i-j,\\
(a^m,b^n)\in\D_L(a^i\xi b^j),\ \xi\in\{c,d\}
 &\Longleftrightarrow
 n-m=i-j\ \text{and}\
 m\equiv\operatorname{par}(\xi)-i\pmod2.
\end{array}
\]
Thus an $e$-factor's distribution is determined by $i-j$, and a $c/d$-factor's
by $(i-j,\operatorname{par}(\xi)-i\bmod2)$.  A shared context makes the
corresponding invariant equal for $x$ and $y$; equal $h_{\pm,e}$-types place
them on the same branch.  Hence $\D_L(x)=\D_L(y)$.

The language is not Clark--Eyraud substitutable because $c,e\in L$ share the
empty context but $(a,b)\in\D_L(e)\setminus\D_L(c)$.  Finally fix
$k,\ell$ and put $N:=2\max\{1,k+\ell\}$,
$w_e:=a^keb^\ell$, $w_c:=a^kcb^\ell$.  These factors have the same
length-$k$ prefix and length-$\ell$ suffix and share
$(a^{N-k},b^{N-\ell})$, whereas
\[
 (a^{N+1-k},b^{N+1-\ell})
 \in\D_L(w_e)\setminus\D_L(w_c).
\]
The \hyperref[crit:kl]{fixed-window counterexample criterion} therefore gives
$L_{\pm,e}\notin\mathsf{KL}$.
\end{proof}

Thus Theorem~\ref{thm:linear-poly} applies to a nonregular linear language
outside $\mathsf{KL}$.  The displayed grammar is even-linear, so no
separation from Takada's control-set framework is claimed.

\section{Expressiveness and Boundaries}
\label{sec:boundaries}

We now locate the framework between the fixed-window hierarchy and broader
context-free families.

\subsection{A nonlinear target inside \texorpdfstring{$\mathsf{RS}$}{RS}}

Let $\Delta:=\{a^nb^n:n\ge0\}$ and consider its Kleene closure $\Delta^*$.
For $z\in\{a,b\}^*$ put $\beta(z):=|z|_a-|z|_b$, and let
$\underline\beta(z)$ be the minimum of $\beta$ over all prefixes of $z$,
including $\lambda$ and $z$ itself.  If $z_i z_{i+1}=ba$, call position $i$
an \emph{occurrence} of $ba$ and call $\beta(z[1..i])$ its height.

We use the following characterization.  \textup{($\star$)} A word $w$ belongs
to $\Delta^*$ iff $\underline\beta(w)\ge0$, $\beta(w)=0$, and every occurrence
of $ba$ in $w$ has height $0$.  The forward implication is immediate from a
factorization $w=a^{n_1}b^{n_1}\cdots a^{n_r}b^{n_r}$.  Conversely, assume the
right-hand side and $w\ne\lambda$.  Cut $w$ at consecutive positions of height
$0$.  Every interior position of each resulting segment has positive height, so
an internal occurrence of $ba$ would contradict the zero-height condition in
\textup{($\star$)}.  Hence each segment lies in $a^*b^*$, and its total balance
is $0$, so it has the form $a^nb^n$.  The case $w=\lambda$ is immediate.

For a word $w$, let $\operatorname{fst}(w),\operatorname{lst}(w)\in
\{\bot,a,b\}$ be its first and last symbols, with both equal to $\bot$ on
$\lambda$, and let $\operatorname{ba}(w)\in\{0,1\}$ record whether $w$
contains $ba$.  Put
$h_\star(w):=(\operatorname{fst}(w),\operatorname{lst}(w),\operatorname{ba}(w))$.
The first symbol of $xy$ is inherited from $x$ when $x\ne\lambda$ and from
$y$ otherwise, and the last symbol is symmetric.  Moreover, $xy$ contains
$ba$ iff $x$ contains $ba$, or $y$ contains $ba$, or
$\operatorname{lst}(x)=b$ and $\operatorname{fst}(y)=a$.  Thus
$h_\star(xy)$ is determined by $h_\star(x)$ and $h_\star(y)$.  On the finite
image $M_\star:=h_\star(\{a,b\}^*)$, define
$h_\star(x)\cdot h_\star(y):=h_\star(xy)$.  This product is well defined;
surjectivity together with associativity of concatenation makes it associative,
and $h_\star(\lambda)$ is the identity.  Hence $M_\star$ is a finite monoid
and $h_\star:\{a,b\}^*\to M_\star$ is a homomorphism.

For a nonempty word $x$ and $d\in\mathbb Z$, call $d$ an
\emph{admissible entry height} for $x$ if
$d+\underline\beta(x)\ge0$ and $d+\eta=0$ for every height $\eta$ of an
internal occurrence of $ba$ in $x$.  Write $\mathcal E(x)$ for the set of
all such $d$.

\begin{proposition}[a nonlinear fixed-$h_\star$ target]
\label{prop:nonlinear-rs-example}
The language $\Delta^*$ is context-free, nonregular, and non-linear, and it is
$\sim_{h_\star}$-substitutable for the finite homomorphism above.  Moreover,
it is not $(k,\ell)$-substitutable for any fixed $k,\ell\ge0$.  In particular,
$\Delta^*\in\mathsf{RS}_{h_\star}(\{a,b\})\cap\mathrm{CFL}$ and
$\Delta^*\in\mathsf{RS}(\{a,b\})\setminus\mathsf{KL}(\{a,b\})$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The grammar $S\to TS\mid\lambda$, $T\to aTb\mid\lambda$ generates
$\Delta^*$, so the language is context-free.  It is nonregular because
$\Delta^*\cap a^*b^*=\Delta$.  It is also non-linear: linear languages are
closed under intersection with regular languages, whereas
$\Delta^*\cap a^*b^*a^*b^*=\Delta\Delta$ is not linear~\cite{autebert1997}.

\emph{$\sim_{h_\star}$-substitutability.}
Let nonempty $x,y$ satisfy $h_\star(x)=h_\star(y)$ and share a context $(u,v)$.
Since $uxv,uyv\in\Delta^*$, comparison of total balances gives
$\beta(x)=\beta(y)$.

Applying \textup{($\star$)} to $u'xv'$ shows that, for a fixed context
$(u',v')$, the membership conditions depending on $x$ are determined by
$\operatorname{fst}(x)$, $\operatorname{lst}(x)$, $\beta(x)$, and whether
$\beta(u')\in\mathcal E(x)$.
Indeed, the prefix-nonnegativity and zero-height conditions internal to $x$ are
precisely encoded by $\beta(u')\in\mathcal E(x)$.  A possible $ba$ at the
boundary $u'|x$ is determined by $\operatorname{fst}(x)$ and $\beta(u')$,
while the boundary $x|v'$ and all height conditions internal to $v'$ depend
only on the exit height $\beta(u')+\beta(x)$ and on
$\operatorname{lst}(x)$.  It
therefore suffices to prove $\mathcal E(x)=\mathcal E(y)$.

If $\operatorname{ba}(x)=\operatorname{ba}(y)=1$, any internal occurrence of
$ba$ at height $\eta$ forces an admissible entry height to equal $-\eta$.
Thus each of $\mathcal E(x)$ and $\mathcal E(y)$ has at most one element.
The shared context gives $\beta(u)\in\mathcal E(x)\cap\mathcal E(y)$, so
$\mathcal E(x)=\{\beta(u)\}=\mathcal E(y)$.

If $\operatorname{ba}(x)=\operatorname{ba}(y)=0$, then $x,y\in a^*b^*$.
For every $z\in a^*b^*$, $\underline\beta(z)=\min\{0,\beta(z)\}$, and hence
$\mathcal E(z)=\{d\in\mathbb Z:d\ge-\min\{0,\beta(z)\}\}$.  Since
$\beta(x)=\beta(y)$, again $\mathcal E(x)=\mathcal E(y)$.  Therefore
$\D_{\Delta^*}(x)=\D_{\Delta^*}(y)$, proving
$\sim_{h_\star}$-substitutability.

\emph{Excluding fixed windows.}
Fix $k,\ell\ge0$ and put
$m_{\mathrm{win}}:=\max\{1,k+\ell\}$,
$s:=a^{m_{\mathrm{win}}}b^{m_{\mathrm{win}}}$, and
$t:=a^{m_{\mathrm{win}}}b^{m_{\mathrm{win}}}
     a^{m_{\mathrm{win}}}b^{m_{\mathrm{win}}}$.  Both words have length at least
$\max\{1,k+\ell\}$, share the length-$k$ prefix $a^k$ and length-$\ell$
suffix $b^\ell$, and share the empty context because $s,t\in\Delta^*$.
Moreover
$asb=a^{m_{\mathrm{win}}+1}b^{m_{\mathrm{win}}+1}\in\Delta^*$,
whereas in
$atb=a^{m_{\mathrm{win}}+1}b^{m_{\mathrm{win}}}
      a^{m_{\mathrm{win}}}b^{m_{\mathrm{win}}+1}$ the central occurrence of $ba$ has height $1$,
so \textup{($\star$)} gives $atb\notin\Delta^*$.  Thus
$(a,b)\in\D_{\Delta^*}(s)\setminus\D_{\Delta^*}(t)$, and the
\hyperref[crit:kl]{fixed-window counterexample criterion} shows that
$\Delta^*$ is not $(k,\ell)$-substitutable.  Since $k,\ell$ were arbitrary,
$\Delta^*\notin\mathsf{KL}(\{a,b\})$.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:linear-separator-example} and this example show two
different ranges of the fixed-$h$ framework.  The former shows that the
polynomial characteristic-data theorem for the linear subclass already applies
to a nonregular linear language outside the fixed-window hierarchy.  In
contrast, $\Delta^*$ shows that the general context-free fixed-$h$ theorem
reaches a non-linear context-free language outside that hierarchy.  In
particular, because $\Delta^*$ is not linear, it is not even-linear either.
No separation from control-set methods in general is claimed here.

\subsection{Regular separation from the fixed-window hierarchy}

Let $\Sigma_c:=\{\uparrow,\downarrow,\#\}$ be the counter alphabet.  In this
subsection, abbreviate $\mathsf{RS}(\Sigma_c)$ and $\mathsf{KL}(\Sigma_c)$ by
$\mathsf{RS}$ and $\mathsf{KL}$.  For
$w=\delta_1\cdots\delta_n\in\Sigma_c^*$ put $\mathrm{ht}_0(w):=0$ and define
$\mathrm{ht}_i(w)$ by reading from left to right: $\uparrow$ increases the
height by $1$, $\downarrow$ decreases it by $1$, and $\#$ resets it to $0$.
For $\rho\ge1$, put
$\mathrm{CTR}_\rho:=\{w\in\Sigma_c^*:0\le\mathrm{ht}_i(w)\le\rho
\text{ for all }0\le i\le|w|\}$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:ctr-regular}
For every $\rho\ge1$, $\mathrm{CTR}_\rho$ is regular and hence belongs to
$\mathsf{RS}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Use states $0,1,\ldots,\rho$ to record the current height and one sink state
after leaving the allowed range.  The symbol $\#$ sends every non-sink state
to $0$.  Thus $\mathrm{CTR}_\rho$ is regular, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:regular-auto} gives membership in $\mathsf{RS}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:ctr-non-kl}
For every $\rho\ge2$, $\mathrm{CTR}_\rho$ is not $(k,\ell)$-substitutable for
any $k,\ell\ge0$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Fix $k,\ell\ge0$ and put
$s:=(\uparrow\downarrow)^k\uparrow^{\rho-1}\#^\ell$ and
$t:=(\uparrow\downarrow)^k\uparrow^\rho\#^\ell$.  Since $\rho\ge2$,
$|s|,|t|\ge\max\{1,k+\ell\}$, and the two words share the same length-$k$
prefix and the same length-$\ell$ suffix $\#^\ell$.

Both $s$ and $t$ lie in $\mathrm{CTR}_\rho$, so they share the empty context.
In $\uparrow s$, after the initial $\uparrow$, the factor
$(\uparrow\downarrow)^k$ runs between heights $1$ and $2$, and the final
$\uparrow^{\rho-1}$ reaches exactly height $\rho$.  Thus
$\uparrow s\in\mathrm{CTR}_\rho$ because $\rho\ge2$.  By contrast,
$\uparrow t$ reaches height $\rho+1$ before the first reset, so
$\uparrow t\notin\mathrm{CTR}_\rho$.  Hence
$(\uparrow,\lambda)\in\D_{\mathrm{CTR}_\rho}(s)
\setminus\D_{\mathrm{CTR}_\rho}(t)$, and the
\hyperref[crit:kl]{fixed-window counterexample criterion} proves the claim.
\end{proof}

For every $\rho\ge2$, Proposition~\ref{prop:regular-auto} supplies a finite
monoid homomorphism $h_\rho$ recognizing $\mathrm{CTR}_\rho$.  Hence
Proposition~\ref{prop:ctr-regular} and Theorem~\ref{thm:ctr-non-kl} give
\[
 \mathrm{CTR}_\rho\in\Ccf{h_\rho}\setminus\mathsf{KL}.
\]
Thus failure of all fixed-window conditions already occurs inside a single
fixed-$h$ learnable slice, and already for a regular target.  The target-dependent
union $\mathsf{RS}$ is used only for expressive comparisons, not as the class
to which the learning theorem is applied.

\subsection{Natural deterministic context-free languages outside \texorpdfstring{$\mathsf{RS}$}{RS}}

The following simple obstruction isolates the common reason why the
counter and Dyck examples below fall outside every class induced by a finite-monoid homomorphism.

\begin{lemma}[finite-monoid obstruction]\label{lem:finite-monoid-obstruction}
Let $L\subseteq\Sigma^*$.  Suppose there is an infinite set
$\Xi\subseteq\Sigma^+$ such that for every distinct $x,y\in\Xi$,
$\D_L(x)\cap\D_L(y)\ne\emptyset$ and $\D_L(x)\ne\D_L(y)$.
Then $L\notin\mathsf{RS}(\Sigma)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $h:\Sigma^*\to M$ be any homomorphism into a finite monoid.  Since
$\Xi$ is infinite and $M$ is finite, there exist distinct $x,y\in\Xi$ with
$h(x)=h(y)$.  Their distributions
intersect but are unequal, contradicting $\sim_h$-substitutability.  Thus
$L\notin\mathsf{RS}_h(\Sigma)$ for every finite $h$.
\end{proof}

Removing the upper cap gives
\[
 \mathrm{CTR}:=
 \{w\in\Sigma_c^*:\mathrm{ht}_i(w)\ge0\text{ for every }0\le i\le |w|\}.
\]
It is deterministic context-free: a deterministic pushdown automaton pushes
on $\uparrow$, pops on $\downarrow$ (rejecting underflow), and clears the
stack to its bottom marker on $\#$.

\begin{corollary}[uncapped counter obstruction]
$\mathrm{CTR}\notin\mathsf{RS}(\Sigma_c)$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:finite-monoid-obstruction} to
$\Xi=\{\uparrow^i:i\ge1\}$.  If $i<j$, then $(\lambda,\downarrow^i)$ is a context of both
$\uparrow^i$ and $\uparrow^j$, while $(\lambda,\downarrow^j)$ is a context
of $\uparrow^j$ but not of $\uparrow^i$.
\end{proof}

The same obstruction appears in the one-bracket Dyck language
\[
 D_1:=\{w\in\{a,b\}^*: |w'|_a\ge|w'|_b
 \text{ for every prefix }w'\text{ of }w,\ 
 |w|_a=|w|_b\},
\]
where $|t|_a$ and $|t|_b$ denote the numbers of $a$'s and $b$'s in a
word $t$.
This is a standard deterministic context-free language~\cite{ginsburg-greibach1966}.
Our symbol $D_1$ denotes the whole one-bracket Dyck language.  In the notation
of Autebert--Berstel--Boasson~\cite{autebert1997}, the Dyck language is the
free submonoid generated by the Dyck-prime language and is written $D_1^*$;
thus our $D_1$ corresponds to their $D_1^*$.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:dyck-not-rs}
$D_1\notin\mathsf{RS}(\{a,b\})$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:finite-monoid-obstruction} to
$\Xi=\{b^i a^i:i\ge1\}$.  For $i<j$, the context $(a^j,b^j)$ is common to
$b^ia^i$ and $b^ja^j$, while $(a^i,b^i)$ is a context of $b^ia^i$ but not
of $b^ja^j$, since the prefix $a^ib^j$ underflows.
\end{proof}
Thus $\mathsf{RS}(\{a,b\})$ does not contain all deterministic context-free
languages over $\{a,b\}$.

This obstruction has a useful pushdown interpretation.  The interesting point
is that unbounded stack behaviour by itself is not incompatible with
$\mathsf{RS}$: $L_{\pm,e}$ is already a nonregular one-turn language in
$\mathsf{RS}(\{a,b,c,d,e\})$.  What fails for $\mathrm{CTR}$ and $D_1$
is more specific: an infinite family of factors remains mutually comparable
through common contexts while carrying pairwise distinct contextual behaviours,
so no finite $h$ can keep all incompatible factors apart.  We use this only as
an obstruction and not as a complete pushdown characterization of
$\mathsf{RS}\cap\mathrm{CFL}$.

We use one elementary closure fact for the final example.
\begin{lemma}[fixed-word quotient]\label{lem:rs-fixed-quotient}
If $L$ is $\sim_h$-substitutable and $z\in\Sigma^*$, then the right
quotient $L/z:=\{w:wz\in L\}$ is $\sim_h$-substitutable.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A common context $(u,v)$ for equal-type nonempty $x,y$ in $L/z$ becomes
$(u,vz)$ in $L$, so $\D_L(x)=\D_L(y)$; replacing any context $(s,t)$ by
$(s,tz)$ then gives $\D_{L/z}(x)=\D_{L/z}(y)$.
\end{proof}

The language generated by $S\to aSS\mid b$ is usually called the
\emph{\L{}ukasiewicz language}.  Autebert--Berstel--Boasson write the
standard relation as $L_{\mathrm{Luk}}=D_1^*b$; with the present notation for
the whole Dyck language this is $L_{\mathrm{Luk}}=D_1b$
~\cite{autebert1997}.  It is also a standard
deterministic context-free language~\cite{ginsburg-greibach1966}.  If
$L_{\mathrm{Luk}}\in\mathsf{RS}(\{a,b\})$, Lemma~\ref{lem:rs-fixed-quotient} would give
$D_1=L_{\mathrm{Luk}}/b\in\mathsf{RS}(\{a,b\})$, contradicting
Corollary~\ref{cor:dyck-not-rs}.


\subsection{Comparison with Clark's congruential family}

Clark's convention~\cite{clark2010congruential} permits a finite set of
initial symbols in place of a single start symbol.  For a finite initial set
$I\subseteq V$, write $L_G[I]:=\bigcup_{A\in I}L_G(A)$.  A CFG $G$ with
initial set $I$ is \emph{congruential} if, for every nonterminal $A$,
$L_G(A)$ is contained in a single syntactic-congruence class of $L_G[I]$.
The condition is vacuous when $L_G(A)=\emptyset$.  For a finite alphabet
$\Gamma$, let $\mathsf{CONG}(\Gamma)$ denote the languages over $\Gamma$
generated by congruential CFGs.  Clark~\cite{clark2010congruential} already
notes that substitutable and $(k,\ell)$-substitutable languages belong to
this congruential family, and uses the Dyck language as a standard example
beyond ordinary substitutability.  The next proposition extends the
containment statement to every fixed-$h$ slice and hence to
$\mathsf{RS}\cap\mathrm{CFL}$; $D_1$ is reused only as a properness witness.

\begin{proposition}[comparison with Clark's congruential family]
\label{prop:clark-congruential-comparison}
For every finite alphabet $\Gamma$ and every finite-monoid homomorphism
$h:\Gamma^*\to M$, we have
$\Ccf{h}\subseteq\mathsf{CONG}(\Gamma)$.  Consequently,
$\mathsf{RS}(\Gamma)\cap\mathrm{CFL}
 \subseteq\mathsf{CONG}(\Gamma)$,
and for $\Gamma=\{a,b\}$ this inclusion is proper.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $L\in\Ccf{h}$.  If $L=\emptyset$, take one initial nonterminal with no
productions.  Assume henceforth that $L\ne\emptyset$.

Take a reduced SSBNF grammar for $L$ and apply the typed refinement of
Section~\ref{sec:typing}, obtaining
$\widetilde G=(\widetilde V,\Gamma,\widetilde P,\widetilde S)$.  Fix an
arbitrary non-start typed nonterminal $X=A_\mu$.  Since $\widetilde G$ is
reduced, $X$ has some terminal reaching context $(u,v)$.  Thus
$uwv\in L$ for every $w\in L_{\widetilde G}(X)$.  On the other hand,
Proposition~\ref{prop:typed-core} gives $h(w)=\mu$, and SSBNF has no
non-start $\lambda$-production, so $w\ne\lambda$.

Now take arbitrary $x,y\in L_{\widetilde G}(X)$.  They are nonempty strings
with the same $h$-value and the common context $(u,v)$.  Since $L$ is
$\sim_h$-substitutable, $\D_L(x)=\D_L(y)$, that is, $x\equiv_L y$.
Hence, for every non-start typed nonterminal $X$,
$L_{\widetilde G}(X)$ is contained in a single $\equiv_L$-class.

Remove $\widetilde S$ and its productions, and let
$I:=\{X:\widetilde S\to X\in\widetilde P\}$ be the initial set.  If
$\lambda\in L$, add a fresh nonterminal $X_\lambda$ with the rule
$X_\lambda\to\lambda$ and include $X_\lambda$ in $I$.  Since
$\widetilde S$ occurs on no right-hand side, this operation does not change
the yield language of any old non-start symbol.  Moreover,
Proposition~\ref{prop:typed-core} gives $L(\widetilde G)=L$, so the resulting
grammar $G'$ satisfies $L_{G'}[I]=L$.  Each old non-start symbol generates
only one $\equiv_L$-class as shown above, and
$L_{G'}(X_\lambda)=\{\lambda\}\subseteq[\lambda]_{\equiv_L}$.  Thus $G'$ is
congruential and $L\in\mathsf{CONG}(\Gamma)$.  This proves
$\Ccf{h}\subseteq\mathsf{CONG}(\Gamma)$, and therefore
$\mathsf{RS}(\Gamma)\cap\mathrm{CFL}
 \subseteq\mathsf{CONG}(\Gamma)$.

For properness, consider the one-bracket Dyck language $D_1$ over
$\Gamma=\{a,b\}$.  For every $x\in D_1$ and $u,v\in\{a,b\}^*$,
$uxv\in D_1$ iff $uv\in D_1$.  Indeed, $x$ has total balance $0$ and every
prefix of $x$ has nonnegative relative balance.  Thus inserting $x$ between
$u$ and $v$ leaves all subsequent heights unchanged and never drops below
the height at the insertion point while traversing $x$; deleting $x$
preserves the same height condition in reverse.  Hence
$x\equiv_{D_1}\lambda$.

Therefore the grammar $S\to aSbS\mid\lambda$ is congruential, since
$L_G(S)=D_1\subseteq[\lambda]_{\equiv_{D_1}}$, and so
$D_1\in\mathsf{CONG}(\{a,b\})$.  On the other hand,
Corollary~\ref{cor:dyck-not-rs} gives
$D_1\notin\mathsf{RS}(\{a,b\})$.  Hence
$\mathsf{RS}(\{a,b\})\cap\mathrm{CFL}
 \subsetneq\mathsf{CONG}(\{a,b\})$.
\end{proof}


\section{Conclusion}

For a fixed finite-monoid typing $h$, every context-free
$\sim_h$-substitutable language is reconstructed exactly from finitely many
canonical positive witnesses, and a conservative learner identifies every
nonempty target in the limit with polynomial-time updates.  The witnesses are
polynomial in grammar size and typed thickness.  For the fixed typing
$h_\circ$, no set-driven operator with characteristic samples for all of
$\Ccf{h_\circ}$ admits a bound polynomial in reduced CFG size and ordinary
thickness, whereas such a bound holds for every fixed prefix--suffix window
and a grammar-size bound holds for linear targets.  Structurally, suitable
fixed-$h$ slices contain regular, nonregular linear, and nonlinear
context-free targets outside every fixed-window class; the regular,
nonregular linear, and nonlinear separation examples use
$\{\uparrow,\downarrow,\#\}$, $\{a,b,c,d,e\}$, and $\{a,b\}$,
respectively.  Moreover, $D_1\notin\mathsf{RS}$, and
$\mathsf{RS}(\Gamma)\cap\mathrm{CFL}$ is properly contained in Clark's
congruential family.

Open problems include characterizing $\mathsf{RS}\cap\mathrm{CFL}$,
choosing useful typings, learning with unknown $h$, extensions to richer
grammar formalisms, and efficiency notions based on other representation or
finite-sample criteria.

\subsection{Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:window-typed-yield}}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:window-typed-yield}]
When $r=0$, $h_{0,0}$ is constant on $\Sigma^+$, so a productive typed
nonterminal $A_\mu$ has the same nonempty yields as its underlying nonterminal
$A$ and hence $|\omega(A_\mu)|\le\tau_G$.

Assume $r>0$ and let $A_\mu$ be productive.  If $\mu$ is a short-word
element of $M_{k,\ell}$, then every word of type $\mu$ is that very word, of
length $<r$, and the required bound is immediate.  It remains to consider a
long-word summary element $\mu=\langle p,q\rangle$.  Choose any derivation
tree of $A$ whose terminal yield $w$ has this type.  Mark the first $k$
terminal leaves and the last $\ell$ terminal leaves; there are exactly $r$
marked leaves, and their labels are $p$ and $q$ in the required order.

Let $T_0$ be the union of the root-to-marked-leaf paths.  Consider each
maximal chain of nonterminal nodes in $T_0$ along which exactly one child
remains in $T_0$.  If the same underlying nonterminal label occurs twice on
such a chain, replace the derivation rooted at the upper occurrence by the
subtree rooted at the lower occurrence.  This is a valid derivation from the
same nonterminal and deletes only sibling subtrees containing no marked leaf,
so all marked boundary terminals and their order are preserved.  Repeating
the shortcut leaves at most $N$ nonterminal nodes on each maximal one-child
chain.

Contract every maximal one-child chain.  The resulting branching skeleton
connects the $r$ marked leaves and has at most $2r-1$ vertices, so there are
at most $2r-1$ maximal one-child chains (including a possible initial chain
above the first branching vertex).  An omitted sibling subtree can occur only
at a node on one of these chains; branching nodes have both children in
$T_0$.  Hence there are at most $(2r-1)N$ omitted sibling subtrees.  Replace
each by a shortest terminal yield of its root, contributing at most $\tau_G$
terminals per subtree.  The resulting word $w'$ is still derived from $A$,
still has prefix $p$ and suffix $q$, and therefore has the same
$h_{k,\ell}$-type $\mu$, while
\[
 |w'|\le r+(2r-1)N\tau_G.
\]
By the same yield-type annotation used in
Proposition~\ref{prop:typed-core}, this derivation lifts to
$A_\mu\Rightarrow_{\widetilde G}^*w'$; since $A_\mu$ is reachable and the tree
is successful, all typed symbols on it survive trimming.  Therefore $w'$ is
a typed yield of $A_\mu$, and the shortlex-minimal typed yield $\omega(A_\mu)$ obeys
the same length bound.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Thickness-preserving SSBNF normalization}
\label{app:thick-ssbnf}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:thick-ssbnf-normal}]
Let $R=(V,\Sigma,P,S)$ be reduced and generate a nonempty language.  Put
$n:=|R|$ and $\bar\tau:=\tau_R+1$.  We describe a standard normalization in
an order that keeps both grammar size and shortest terminal yields polynomial.

First introduce a fresh start symbol $S_0$ with $S_0\to S$ and, if
$\lambda\in L(R)$, retain the possibility $S_0\to\lambda$ at the end.  Isolate every terminal occurring in a right-hand side of length at least two
by a fresh wrapper $W_a\to a$, and binarize every right-hand side of length
greater than two.
Call the resulting grammar $G_{\mathrm{bin}}$.  These operations have total size $O(n)$ up
to the chosen reasonable encoding.  Every original nonterminal keeps the same
terminal language.  Every fresh binarization symbol expands a contiguous block
of some original right-hand side, containing at most $O(n)$ original symbols;
replacing each productive nonterminal in that block by a shortest terminal
yield gives
\[
 \tau_{G_{\mathrm{bin}}}\le c_1 n\bar\tau
\]
for a fixed constant $c_1$.

We next bound the shortest \emph{nonempty} yield of a nullable symbol of $G_{\mathrm{bin}}$.
Suppose $A\Rightarrow_{G_{\mathrm{bin}}}^*w$ for some nonempty terminal word $w$.  Choose one
terminal leaf in a derivation tree and follow the path from the root to that
leaf.  If the same nonterminal occurs twice on this path, replace the subtree
at the upper occurrence by the subtree at the lower occurrence.  Repeating
this shortcut yields a path with at most $|V_{G_{\mathrm{bin}}}|$ nonterminal occurrences.
At every binary node on the path, replace the off-path sibling by a shortest
terminal yield; a nullable sibling may contribute the empty word.  The chosen
terminal leaf contributes one symbol.  Hence
\[
 \min\{\,|w|>0:A\Rightarrow_{G_{\mathrm{bin}}}^*w\,\}
 \le 1+|V_{G_{\mathrm{bin}}}|\tau_{G_{\mathrm{bin}}}
 \le c_2 n^2\bar\tau
\]
for another fixed constant $c_2$.

Now eliminate all non-start $\lambda$-productions.  Because $G_{\mathrm{bin}}$ is binary, a
rule $A\to BC$ creates at most the variants $A\to BC$, $A\to B$, and
$A\to C$ according to which children are nullable; there is therefore no
exponential nullable-subset expansion.  The usual construction preserves, for
every nonterminal, its nonempty terminal language, so the preceding bound is
unchanged.  Next compute unit closure and eliminate non-start unit productions.
For each source nonterminal and each unit-reachable non-unit production, copy
that production to the source.  With $O(n)$ nonterminals and $O(n)$
non-unit productions before this step, this creates at most $O(n^2)$ rules.
Unit elimination preserves every nonterminal language and therefore does not
increase the thickness bound.  Finally trim useless symbols.

The resulting grammar is reduced and has separated start form; every non-start
rule is terminal or binary, and $S_0\to\lambda$ is the only possible
$\lambda$-rule.  Thus it is in SSBNF.  Its size is polynomial in $n$, and
its thickness is at most $c_2 n^2(\tau_R+1)$.  All transformations above are
computable in polynomial time, proving the proposition.
\end{proof}

\section{Proof of the linear-spine SSBNF normalization}
\label{app:linear-normalization}

The construction for Proposition~\ref{prop:linear-normal} combines standard
start-symbol separation, elimination of useless, nullable, and unit rules,
and binarization, with the cases written out to preserve the single-spine form
needed in Section~\ref{sec:linear}.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:linear-normal}]
If $L(G)=\{\lambda\}$, take the reduced grammar with the single rule
$S_0\to\lambda$; it has no binary productions, so the required condition is
vacuous.  Hence assume below that $L(G)$ contains a nonempty word.

Let $S$ be the original start symbol.  Introduce a fresh $S_0$ that never
occurs on a right-hand side, with $S_0\to S$ and, if $\lambda\in L(G)$,
$S_0\to\lambda$.  Treat $S$ thereafter as a non-start symbol.  Remove useless
symbols, eliminate non-start $\lambda$-rules while preserving $\lambda$ only
at $S_0$, and then eliminate non-start unit rules.  Unit elimination is not
applied to the start rules, so every start rule surviving final trimming has
the required form $S_0\to A$ or $S_0\to\lambda$.  This order also removes unit rules created by $\lambda$-elimination.
Linearity implies that nullable elimination creates at most one additional
alternative per production and leaves at most one nonterminal on every
surviving right-hand side.  Unit closure copies at most one non-unit
production for each source/reachable-production pair, so these preprocessing
steps remain polynomial.

It remains to binarize without creating a new unit rule.  Introduce one fresh
wrapper $W_a\to a$ for each terminal $a$ used as a binary child below.
For a rule $A\to uBv$, write $u=a_1\cdots a_m$ and
$v=b_1\cdots b_n$.  Because non-start unit rules have already been
eliminated, the case $m=n=0$ (which would be the unit rule $A\to B$) cannot
occur; hence $m+n>0$.  Treat the cases explicitly.  If $m=0$, then necessarily
$n>0$.  For $n=1$ use $A\to BW_{b_1}$.
For $n\ge2$, introduce fresh $Q_1,\ldots,Q_{n-1}$ and use
\[
 Q_1\to BW_{b_1},\qquad
 Q_j\to Q_{j-1}W_{b_j}\ (2\le j\le n-1),\qquad
 A\to Q_{n-1}W_{b_n}.
\]
Now suppose $m>0$.  Define $U:=B$ if $n=0$.  Here $B$ is a
nonterminal of the pre-wrapper grammar, so it is not one of the fresh
terminal wrappers introduced in this binarization.  If $n>0$, introduce fresh
$Q_1,\ldots,Q_n$ and set
\[
 Q_1\to BW_{b_1},\qquad
 Q_j\to Q_{j-1}W_{b_j}\quad(2\le j\le n),
\]
then put $U:=Q_n$.  For $m=1$ use the single binary rule $A\to W_{a_1}U$.
For $m\ge2$, introduce fresh $J_1,\ldots,J_{m-1}$ and use
\[
 A\to W_{a_1}J_1,\qquad
 J_i\to W_{a_{i+1}}J_{i+1}\quad(1\le i\le m-2),\qquad
 J_{m-1}\to W_{a_m}U.
\]
Thus every case $m=0$ or $m\ge1$, $n=0$ or $n\ge1$ with $m+n>0$ is covered,
and the innermost binary rule contains $B$ itself rather than a fresh unit
rule.

For a terminal-only rule $A\to a_1\cdots a_m$, keep $A\to a_1$ when
$m=1$.  For $m\ge2$, introduce fresh
$\Theta_2,\ldots,\Theta_m$ and use
\[
 A\to W_{a_1}\Theta_2,\qquad
 \Theta_i\to W_{a_i}\Theta_{i+1}\quad(2\le i\le m-1),\qquad
 \Theta_m\to a_m.
\]
(When $m=2$, the middle family is empty.)  Every new non-start rule is
therefore either terminal or binary with exactly one wrapper child and one
other nonterminal child.  By construction, a wrapper $W_a$ occurs only in the
wrapper-child position of a binary rule and never as its continuing
nonterminal child; the terminal-only construction ends instead in a fresh
non-wrapper chain symbol with a terminal rule.  Final trimming preserves this property,
the separated start form, and reducedness.  The wrapper and chain
factorizations are linear in the already polynomial total right-hand-side
length, so the whole construction has polynomial size and runs in polynomial
time.
\end{proof}

\section{Proof of the short canonical-yield lemma}
\label{app:linear-short-proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:linear-short}]
In the linear-spine SSBNF form of
Proposition~\ref{prop:linear-normal}, each binary spine step has the form
$A\to W_aB$ or $A\to BW_a$, with $W_a\Rightarrow a$, and therefore emits
exactly one terminal outside the continuing nonterminal.

If $X$ is a terminal-wrapper symbol then $|\omega(X)|=1$.  Otherwise choose
a shortest terminal derivation from the typed spine symbol $X$ and write its
spine as $X=X_0,X_1,\ldots,X_t$, where $X_t$ uses a terminal rule.  Its
yield has length $t+1$.  No typed spine symbol can repeat: if
$X_i=X_j$ with $i<j$, deleting the segment between the two occurrences gives
a valid derivation with fewer spine steps and hence a shorter yield.  Thus the
$t+1$ typed spine symbols are distinct non-start symbols, so
$t+1\le n_t$.  Hence $|\omega(X)|\le n_t$ in all cases.
\end{proof}

\section{Substitutability of the level-coded tree languages}
\label{app:level-coded-substitutability}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:level-coded-substitutable}]
View $\mathtt l,\mathtt r$ as matching tree brackets.  A residual-height-$0$
node is either the clean leaf with body $\mathtt a$ or the shortcut with body
$s_0$; a residual-height-$i\ge1$ node is either the shortcut with body $s_i$
or a binary node with two ordered children of residual height $i-1$.
Serialization surrounds each node by $\mathtt l,\mathtt r$.  Hence
$\mathcal T_i$ is exactly the set of serializations of residual-height-$i$
trees, and $z_i$ is the unique serialization containing no shortcut.

Every word in $\mathcal T_n$ has a unique parse.  At height $0$, the first
body symbol is $\mathtt a$ in the clean case and $\mathtt c$ in the shortcut
case; at positive height it is $\mathtt l$ in the binary case and
$\mathtt c$ in the shortcut case.  In the binary case bracket matching
uniquely separates the two children, so induction determines the whole tree.
The shortcut nodes therefore form a uniquely determined antichain: choosing a
shortcut removes the entire subtree rooted there.

For $i\ge1$ put $b_i:=z_{i-1}z_{i-1}$ and put $b_0:=\mathtt a$.
Thus $s_i=\mathtt c\mathtt0^{i+1}\mathtt d$ is the shortcut body and
$b_i$ is the clean body of a residual-height-$i$ node.  In a valid
serialization, a literal occurrence of either body is the body of a genuine
residual-height-$i$ parsed node.  For $s_i$, this follows because shortcut
bodies contain no brackets and the zero run records the level.  For $b_i$,
bracket matching together with induction identifies the two clean children
when $i\ge1$, while $b_0=\mathtt a$ is the clean leaf body.  Consequently
the literal body replacement
\[
 s_i\longleftrightarrow b_i
\]
is legal exactly at a residual-height-$i$ node, with the common surrounding
$\mathtt l,\mathtt r$ left in place, and stays inside $\mathcal T_n$.

Now suppose $uxv,uyv\in\mathcal T_n$.  The relevant locality statement is
at the level of node bodies.  A differing node itself need not lie wholly
between the two cuts: the left cut may occur immediately after its common
opening $\mathtt l$, or the right cut immediately before its common closing
$\mathtt r$.  Nevertheless every body on which the two parses differ lies
wholly inside the displayed factor.  Indeed, if a differing body crossed the
left cut, its first differing symbol ($\mathtt c$ versus $\mathtt a$ or
$\mathtt l$) would already occur in the common prefix; if it crossed the
right cut, its last differing symbol ($\mathtt d$ versus $\mathtt a$ or
$\mathtt r$) would occur in the common suffix.  Starting from $uxv$, first
expand shortcut bodies present only in its parse and then introduce shortcut
bodies present only in the parse of $uyv$.  All body replacements occur
inside the displayed factor and transform $x$ into $y$.

If $pxq\in\mathcal T_n$, the same literal body-replacement sequence can be
performed inside that occurrence of $x$: the occurrence-recognition argument
above makes every step a legal parsed-node replacement, so the result is
$pyq\in\mathcal T_n$.  Thus
$\D_{\mathcal T_n}(x)\subseteq\D_{\mathcal T_n}(y)$, and reversing the
sequence gives equality.  Therefore $\mathcal T_n$ is substitutable.
\end{proof}
