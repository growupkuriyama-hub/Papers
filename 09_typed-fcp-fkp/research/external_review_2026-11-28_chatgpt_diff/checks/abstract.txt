Distributional approaches to syntax name a category in one of two finite
ways: by a few sentence frames in which its members occur, or by a few
exemplars, the category then consisting of the expressions that fit every
frame shared by the exemplars. Kanazawa and Yoshinaka (2017) identified
the finite context and kernel properties under which the corresponding
learners succeed. We show that the two ways can differ exponentially. For
structures built by binary combination and unary marking with exactly $n$
levels on every branch, require at least one mark. In the number of nonterminals and the lengths
of right-hand sides and witnesses, some grammar names each of its $n+1$
categories by one frame of length at most $2n$, with right-hand sides of
length at most $3n$ but exponentially many productions, whereas for every
exemplar presentation the product of the three is exponential in $n$,
optimal up to a factor $O(n)$; this yields exponential data
bounds for specific learners. In total length, counting all productions
and witnesses, a fixed observer recording whether an expression is
marked permits a frame presentation of total length $O(n^2)$, while every
exemplar presentation, under any observer, has exponential total length;
without an observer separating $\Lce\ta\Rce$ from $\Lfl\ta\ta\Rfl$, frame
presentations need exponential total length too. Both bounds extend
to general one-word deletions, the lower one in terms of a prefix length. Refining the observer can only help
frames and only hurt exemplars for some, but not all, of the finite context
and kernel properties.
