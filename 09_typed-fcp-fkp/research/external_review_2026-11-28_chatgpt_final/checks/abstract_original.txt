Distributional approaches to syntax name a category in two finite ways: by a
few sentence frames in which its members occur, or by a few exemplars, the
category then consisting of the expressions that fit every frame shared by
the exemplars.  Kanazawa and Yoshinaka (2017) identified the finite context
and kernel properties under which the corresponding learners succeed.  We
show that the two kinds of presentation of one language can differ
exponentially.  Our example is an abstract licensing condition:
structures built by binary combination and unary marking, with exactly $n$
levels on every branch, must contain a mark.  Measured by the number of
nonterminals and the lengths of right-hand sides and witnesses, frames need
only $n+1$ categories, each named by one frame of length at most $2n$, and
right-hand sides of length at most $3n$ (though exponentially many
productions); for exemplars the product of the three quantities is at least
exponential in $n$, tight up to a factor $O(n)$.  Hence specific learners
need exponentially much data.  In total length, an
observer recording whether an expression is marked admits a frame
presentation of size $O(n^2)$, whereas every exemplar presentation needs
exponential size under any observer; without an observer that distinguishes
a marked leaf from an unmarked pair of leaves, frame presentations are
exponential too.  The frame upper and exemplar lower bounds extend to
suitable one-word deletions.  Refining the observer can only help frames and
only hurt exemplars for some, but not all, of the finite context and kernel
properties.
